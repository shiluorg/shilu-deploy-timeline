Liu Bei: From a Wandering Leader to Emperor in Chengdu

Story

Aiding Xuzhou, entering Shu, taking Yizhou, contesting Jingzhou, and founding his state in Chengdu

This person-centered line runs from Liu Bei's aid to Xuzhou in 194 to his retreat after the crushing defeat at Xiaoting in 222, in eight chapters. It does not retell the campaigns of Red Cliffs or Hanzhong; it follows Liu Bei's own stages: early relief of Tao Qian under Tian Kai with a following of his own, entering Shu at invitation and breaking with Liu Zhang, capturing Chengdu and holding Yizhou, drawing a boundary with Sun Quan over Jingzhou, proclaiming himself emperor in Chengdu, campaigning against Wu, and ending in the defeat at Xiaoting. The line shows how a long-dependent, wandering faction turned borrowed ground and the conquest of Shu into its own regime, through borrowing land, entering Shu, and claiming the throne.

After decades of wandering, Liu Bei turned borrowed ground and the conquest of Shu into his own regime, ending with the imperial title in Chengdu.

The story, chapter by chapter

  1. 01194 CE

    Aid to Xuzhou and his first mark

    Tao Qian, Governor of Xuzhou, was besieged by Cao Cao and sought help from Tian Kai, Inspector of Qingzhou. Tian Kai sent Liu Bei with his troops to Xuzhou. Liu Bei had only about 1,000 men and a few thousand Xianbei cavalry, but he fought bravely, earning great fame. Tao Qian gave Liu Bei 4,000 Danyang soldiers, strengthening his forces.

    Early dependence: relieving Tao Qian under Tian Kai, beginning to build a following.

    In 194, Tao Qian, Governor of Xuzhou, was besieged by Cao Cao and sought help from Tian Kai, Inspector of Qingzhou. The event record says Tian Kai sent Liu Bei with his troops to relieve Xuzhou.

    Liu Bei had only about a thousand men, together with a few thousand Wuhuan cavalry. The record states he fought bravely and earned great fame; Tao Qian then gave him four thousand Danyang soldiers, strengthening Liu Bei's forces.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter is the early phase when Liu Bei depended on others and began to form a following of his own. He still belonged to Tian Kai's faction, and the reinforcement from Xuzhou was the first regular body of Danyang troops he obtained. The record does not expand on his subsequent movements in the Xuzhou direction.

    Borrowing ground in Shu: leaving Jingzhou and marching upstream to Jiameng Pass.

    In 211, Liu Zhang, Governor of Yizhou, heeding the advice of Zhang Song and Fa Zheng, invited Liu Bei to lead his army into Shu to help attack Zhang Lu of Hanzhong. The event record says Liu Bei left Zhuge Liang and Guan Yu to defend Jingzhou and took Pang Tong, Huang Zhong, Wei Yan, and over twenty thousand men up the Yangtze.

    Liu Zhang himself welcomed Liu Bei at Fucheng, gave him additional troops and grain, and ordered him to garrison Jiameng Pass, in present-day Guangyuan, Sichuan. The record states Liu Bei did not immediately attack Zhang Lu but instead won hearts and minds across Shu.

    In the person-centered line, this is the step from dependence toward holding Shu: Liu Bei left his rear in Jingzhou to his subordinates and stationed himself within Yizhou at Jiameng Pass. The record does not detail the supply or deployment of the invading force.

    From guest to host: removing the Baishui commanders and holding Fucheng.

    In 213, Zhang Song's conspiracy to help Liu Bei was exposed. The event record says Liu Zhang executed Zhang Song and ordered all passes not to let Liu Bei through.

    Following Pang Tong's plan, Liu Bei, under the pretext of returning to Jingzhou, summoned Yang Huai and Gao Pei, commanders of the Baishui garrison, accused them of disrespect, executed them, and absorbed their troops. He then marched to Fucheng, in present-day Mianyang, Sichuan, defeating the forces of Liu Zhang's subordinates Liu Gui and Zhang Ren.

    This chapter marks Liu Bei's shift from guest army to master of Shu. The record states he established a foothold in Yizhou and began besieging Luocheng. It does not detail the reorganization of the absorbed Baishui force or the response of local garrisons.

    Securing the base: besieging Chengdu into surrender and taking the governorship.

    Liu Bei besieged Luocheng for a year, and Pang Tong was hit by a stray arrow and killed. The event record says Liu Bei then summoned Zhuge Liang, Zhang Fei, and Zhao Yun to reinforce him in Shu; after Luocheng fell, he advanced to besiege Chengdu.

    At that time Ma Chao came over from Zhang Lu and camped north of the city, spreading fear throughout Chengdu. Under the persuasion of Qiao Zhou and others, Liu Zhang opened the gates and surrendered. Liu Bei thus took possession of Yizhou, proclaimed himself Governor of Yizhou, and employed Shu talents such as Fa Zheng, Li Yan, Huang Quan, and Liu Ba.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter secures his base of power. The record notes that this achieved the first step of the Longzhong Plan: occupying Jing and Yi. The record does not detail the siege engagements or the internal arrangement of the city.

  5. 05215 CE

    A rift in the alliance: division along the Xiang River, eased on the surface but unresolved.

    After Red Cliffs, Liu Bei had borrowed the Nan commandery, but Sun Quan believed Jingzhou belonged to Eastern Wu. The event record says Sun Quan sent Zhuge Jin, Zhuge Liang's elder brother, to Chengdu to demand the return of Changsha, Guiyang, and Lingling commanderies.

    Liu Bei refused, saying he would return Jingzhou after taking Liangzhou. Sun Quan was furious and sent Lü Meng to seize the three commanderies, while Liu Bei sent Guan Yu to contest them. Then, because Cao Cao attacked Hanzhong and Liu Bei worried about the safety of Yizhou, he negotiated a truce with Sun Quan. They divided Jingzhou along the Xiang River: Changsha, Jiangxia, and Guiyang to Sun Quan; Nan, Lingling, and Wuling to Liu Bei.

    This chapter shows the rift within the alliance was not truly healed. The record presents a surface settlement by boundary: the dispute over western Jingzhou remained, leaving a source for later conflict.

  6. 06221 CE

    The regime takes shape: claiming Han legitimacy and the Zhangwu era.

    In the fourth month of 221, urged on by Zhuge Liang and his other ministers, Liu Bei raised an altar south of Mount Wudan at Chengdu and took the imperial title, keeping "Han" as the dynasty name; historians call it Shu Han. The event record says he had heard reports that Cao Pi had usurped the throne and that Emperor Xian had been killed; in fact Xian had merely been demoted to Duke of Shanyang and was still alive. Liu Bei held mourning for him, gave him the posthumous name Xiaomin, and presented himself as the rightful heir of the Han.

    He proclaimed the Zhangwu era and appointed Zhuge Liang chancellor, Xu Jing minister over the masses, Zhang Fei General of Chariots and Cavalry, and Ma Chao General of Agile Cavalry. The record states the three-way division between Wei, Shu, and Wu was now fixed.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter forms the Liu Bei regime: after borrowing land and entering Shu, he founded his state under the Han name and took the throne. The record does not detail the accession rites or the recognition extended by other powers.

    Campaign against Wu

    After the throne: eastward campaign in the name of avenging Guan Yu.

    After proclaiming himself emperor in Chengdu, Liu Bei, ignoring the remonstrances of Zhao Yun, Zhuge Liang, and others, personally led a campaign against Eastern Wu in the name of avenging Guan Yu. The event record says he commanded the Shu elite eastward; Zhang Fei was murdered by his own subordinates just before the campaign set out, and the accompanying generals included Wu Ban, Feng Xi, and Huang Quan.

    The Shu army advanced east along the Yangtze, capturing Wu County and Zigui. Sun Quan sent Zhuge Jin to sue for peace, but Liu Bei refused. Sun Quan was forced to declare himself a vassal of Cao Pi and appointed Lu Xun Grand Commander to meet the invasion. Troop figures in the record are reported as given in the event record, not independently verified here.

    In the person-centered line, this is a national eastward move after the throne. Liu Bei's aim was both to avenge Guan Yu and related to the ownership of Jingzhou; the internal remonstrances before the campaign are already recorded in the event ledger.

    A setback to the cause: defeated at Xiaoting, entrusting his son and dying soon after.

    Liu Bei's invasion force encamped in a chain from Wuxia to Xiaoting, southeast of present-day Yichang, Hubei. The event record says Lu Xun held fast and refused battle; when the Shu army's morale had drained and the weather turned hot, he ordered a fire attack. The Wu forces set fire downwind, burning over forty Shu camps.

    The Shu army was utterly routed, with tens of thousands killed or wounded, and almost all their boats and equipment destroyed. Liu Bei fled to Baidi City, in present-day Fengjie, Chongqing, and renamed it Yong'an. In the fourth month of the following year, seriously ill, he summoned Zhuge Liang and entrusted his son to him; the record preserves the saying that if the heir were unworthy, Zhuge Liang could take the throne himself. Liu Bei died, and Liu Shan succeeded him.

    This chapter is the setback to Liu Bei's cause: the eastern expedition ended in crushing defeat, and he withdrew to Baidi City until his death. The record does not detail the local terrain of the Xiaoting battlefield or the schedule of the retreat.

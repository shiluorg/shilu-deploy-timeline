The American Revolution: From Stamp Duty to Constitution

In 1765 Parliament passed the Stamp Act, and the North American colonies protested through their first intercolonial congress; in 1773 the Boston Tea Party turned conflict into open confrontation; in 1775 shots were fired at Lexington; in 1776 the Declaration of Independence was signed; in 1777 Saratoga brought France into the war; in 1781 Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown; in 1787 the Constitutional Convention drafted a constitution in Philadelphia. Together these are not an inevitable road to freedom but a series of choices, compromises and exclusions.

Civilizations of the Americas: Caral, Maya and the Inca

Around 2700 BCE the coastal Peruvian site of Caral began building pyramid platforms; around 1400 BCE the Olmec civilisation rose in Mesoamerica; in 100 CE Teotihuacan became one of the earliest great cities of the Americas; in 250 the Maya Classic period began; in 1325 Tenochtitlan was founded on a lake; in 1450 the Inca built Machu Picchu; in 1521 the Spanish captured Tenochtitlan. Together these show how cities and states arose in the Americas without horses, iron or wheeled vehicles, through human labour, water management, terraces and trade.

Assyria and Neo-Babylon: Empire, Library and Jerusalem

In the ninth century BCE Assyria turned from a northern highland state into a war machine across Mesopotamia: in 885 Ashurnasirpal II crushed Aramean revolts; in 880 he moved the capital to Nimrud; in 745 Tiglath-Pileser III built the Neo-Assyrian Empire; in 722 Sargon II founded Dur-Sharrukin; in 655 Ashurbanipal established a great library of clay tablets at Nineveh; in 612 Nineveh fell to a Median-Babylonian coalition. Together these show how empire ruled through armies, deportation, roads and writing — and how its violence was preserved in its own records.

Baghdad: Translation, Algebra and the Golden Age of Medicine

In 751 CE, Tang papermakers captured after the Battle of Talas carried papermaking into Samarkand; Arabs soon established paper works in Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo. In the later eighth century al-Mansur and Harun al-Rashid supported a translation movement that rendered Greek, Persian and Indian works in philosophy, medicine, mathematics and astronomy into Arabic. In 775 Baghdad established the House of Wisdom, where observatories, libraries and a medical school gathered scholars; in 803 the first official paper mill appeared in Baghdad, making paper cheaper and books less costly. In 807 al-Khwarizmi entered the House of Wisdom; in 816 he completed the Sindhind Zij, and in 825 wrote the Algebra, systematising Indian numerals and algebraic methods. In 818 the translation movement reached its peak with key works of Aristotle, Ptolemy and Hippocrates; in 829 al-Farghani completed the Elements of Astronomy, later a European university textbook. Around 902 al-Razi became director of the Baghdad hospital and promoted clinical observation and case records; in 910 he wrote On Smallpox and Measles, separating the two diseases; he died in 925, and his Continens was translated into Latin in 1279 and shaped European medical teaching into the seventeenth century. This story uses twelve nodes to show how a knowledge centre was built: not by one genius, but by paper, translation, institutions and reusable methods.

Byzantium: Walls, Code and Hagia Sophia

In 324 Constantine founded New Rome on the old site of Byzantium by the Bosporus, and the city was inaugurated in 330; in 413 and 447 the Theodosian Walls enclosed it in two stages; in 438 the Theodosian Code was published; in 451 the Council of Chalcedon affirmed two natures in Christ; from 529 to 565 Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis was compiled; in 537 Hagia Sophia was completed. Six events correspond to a city's site, defence, law, religion and symbolism.

Cao Cao: From Chancellor of Jinan to King of Wei

This person-centered line runs from Cao Cao's appointment as Chancellor of Jinan in 187 to his enfeoffment as King of Wei in 216, in eight chapters. It does not retell the campaigns of Guandu or Red Cliffs; it follows Cao Cao's own stages: early local administration, incorporating the Qingzhou Yellow Turbans as armed force, holding Yan Province as a base, advancing on Xuzhou, campaigning against Zhang Xiu, eliminating Lü Bu, and finally turning his power into a fief with its own ancestral temple and bureaucracy through the Duke and King of Wei steps. The line shows how a mobile war faction took root in court legitimacy, grain and manpower, and institutional personnel.

Red Cliffs: The Blocker on the Yangtze

In 208 Cao Cao campaigned south against Jingzhou; Liu Cong surrendered the province, and after Liu Bei was routed at Changban he withdrew to Xiakou and joined Eastern Wu. The Sun–Liu alliance stopped Cao's forces with a fire attack at Red Cliffs, then sealed the alliance by marriage, contested Jiangling, and took the southern commanderies. After Zhou Yu's early death Cao Cao turned to Guanzhong, while Sun and Liu held the Yangtze line in long stalemate. This story follows seven event nodes to show how river warfare and alliance politics held Cao Cao's unifying momentum to the north bank; read the chapters in chronological order.

The Franks: from Clovis's baptism to Verdun

In 486 Clovis expelled Syagrius, the last Roman ruler in Gaul, from Soissons and by his death in 511 controlled most of Gaul; in 496 or 508 he was baptised at Reims, becoming one of the first Germanic kings to convert to Catholic Christianity. In 687 Pippin of Herstal defeated the Neustrians at Tertry and power passed from the Merovingian kings to the mayor of the palace; in 768 Pippin died and the kingdom was divided between Charles and Carloman, with Charles taking the whole realm in 771; in 800 Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne “emperor of the Romans” in Rome; in 843 the Treaty of Verdun divided the empire into middle, eastern and western shares.

The Greek Polis: The Limits of Democracy

In the eighth century BCE the Greek world consisted of hundreds of independent poleis centred on a town. In 776 BCE the poleis held a festival at Olympia and agreed a sacred truce; in 594 BCE Solon cancelled debts and debt-slavery; in 508 BCE Cleisthenes redrew tribes and constituencies, and Athenian citizen politics took shape. In 490 BCE Athens defeated a larger Persian force at Marathon; in 480 BCE the stand at Thermopylae and the naval battle at Salamis turned the war; in 479 BCE Plataea ended the land threat. After victory, Athens turned the Delian League into its own empire, and in 447 BCE the Parthenon began to rise on the Acropolis. In 431 BCE the Peloponnesian War broke out; in 404 BCE Athens surrendered. This story uses twelve nodes to trace what the polis created, and also the exclusions beyond the citizen body, the hierarchy inside an alliance and the self-destruction of war.

Guandu: The Handover of Northern Power

In 196 CE Cao Cao brought Emperor Xian to Xuchang and placed the court behind his own lines. Three years later Yuan Shao swallowed Gongsun Zan and held the four provinces north of the Yellow River; on paper he was still the stronger man. At Guandu in 200, short of troops and grain, Cao Cao cut the Wuchao supply depot; Yuan's sons then fell to fighting and the north changed hands. This story follows eight event nodes to show how logistics and political standing gradually reversed the balance in the north; read the chapters in chronological order.

Hanzhong: How the Triangle Solidified into Tripartite Rule

From 217 Cao Cao and Liu Bei stalemated along the Qinling line; after Xiahou Yuan's death at Dingjun Mountain Liu Bei held Hanzhong, and Guan Yu then flooded seven armies at Fancheng, shaking the Central Plains. Lü Meng crossed in merchant guise to seize Jiangling; Guan Yu fell, and Jingzhou passed to Wu. Liu Bei then became King of Hanzhong, Cao Cao died at Luoyang, and Cao Pi founded Wei. This story follows seven event nodes to show how the Hanzhong, Jingzhou, and Central Plains lines intertwined, turning the earlier momentum toward unification into a three-way balance of Wei, Shu, and Wu; read the chapters in chronological order.

Israel and Judah: Temple, Schism and Exile

Around 1000 BCE David is presented in traditional narrative as the king who united the twelve tribes and made Jerusalem his capital; in 975 Solomon began the First Temple; it was completed in 966; in 958 the Queen of Sheba visited Solomon; in 931 the kingdom split into Israel and Judah after Solomon’s death; in 732 Assyria captured Samaria and the northern kingdom fell. Together these are not a complete “biblical history” but the story of small kingdoms using temple, kingship and text to build memory between the empires of Egypt, Assyria and Babylon.

Indus and Vedic: Cities, Trade and the Origins of Caste

The Indus civilisation evolved from irrigated villages, with nuclear dates around 2600–1900 BCE. Harappa and Mohenjo-daro developed grid streets, drainage, public baths and standard brick houses into an urban order without a clear palace. Trade networks reached Mesopotamia, though specific ports and cargo lists remain disputed. By the middle of the 2nd millennium BCE the urban system was dissolving at different times and in different ways. The Vedic society that followed was not a direct continuation; its varna order gradually fixed in the late Vedic period: Brahmans, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas and Shudras.

Japan's Ritsuryo State: Buddhism, Prince Shotoku and Nara

In 552 King Seong of Baekje sent Buddhist scriptures, an image, and liturgical ornaments to the Japanese court; 538 is also cited. Prince Shōtoku became regent in 593 and later instituted the twelve court ranks; in 604 he issued the Seventeen-Article Constitution. After the 645 coup, the reform rescript declared land and people public; the Taihō code was compiled in 701; in 710 the capital moved to Heijō-kyō, in 741 the kokubunji system was established, and in 752 the Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji was dedicated.

The Three Kingdoms of Korea: Buddhism, the Han River and Unified Silla

In 372 CE, influenced by China, Buddhism entered Goguryeo as ideological backing for a centralising bureaucracy; in the late 5th century Goguryeo took Baekje's Han River territory, Baekje moved its capital to Ungjin and allied with Silla; in 642 Yeon Gaesomun killed the Goguryeo king and took power as regent; in 660 and 668 Silla used Tang armies to destroy Baekje and Goguryeo, then fought nearly a decade to expel Tang; around 751 Kim Dae-seong began Bulguksa and Seokguram. Together these nodes show how three kingdoms competed through Buddhism, relocation, alliance and war, and how they rewrote “unification” after Tang intervention.

The Kushans: Gold, Gandhara and Silk Road Buddhism

In the first century CE the Kushans rose from the Yuezhi confederation; in 37 Kujula Kadphises took the title of king; around 60 Vima Kadphises issued gold coinage, linking the empire’s trade and money to Rome, India and Central Asia; in 78 Kanishka I ascended and the empire reached its height; in 102 Gandharan Buddhist art peaked; around 110 Buddhist art entered China through the Western Regions; in 143 Kushan culture and art reached their height. Together these show a “crossroads empire” joining civilisations through gold, cities and Buddhist art.

Liu Bei: From a Wandering Leader to Emperor in Chengdu

This person-centered line runs from Liu Bei's aid to Xuzhou in 194 to his retreat after the crushing defeat at Xiaoting in 222, in eight chapters. It does not retell the campaigns of Red Cliffs or Hanzhong; it follows Liu Bei's own stages: early relief of Tao Qian under Tian Kai with a following of his own, entering Shu at invitation and breaking with Liu Zhang, capturing Chengdu and holding Yizhou, drawing a boundary with Sun Quan over Jingzhou, proclaiming himself emperor in Chengdu, campaigning against Wu, and ending in the defeat at Xiaoting. The line shows how a long-dependent, wandering faction turned borrowed ground and the conquest of Shu into its own regime, through borrowing land, entering Shu, and claiming the throne.

The Mongol Empire: From the Onon to the Volga

In 1206 Temujin was proclaimed Genghis Khan at the source of the Onon River. He divided the steppe population into ninety-five thousands and drew ten thousand sons of noble families into the keshig guard. Over the next thirty-six years Mongol armies pressured Western Xia, broke the Jin central capital, turned west against Khwarazm, pursued the campaign into the Rus' steppe, and reached Central Europe in 1241. In 1242 news of Ögedei's death brought the western army back from Hungary and Poland to the lower Volga. This story links twelve nodes along that line, attending to organization, mounted archery and coordinated columns, but also to the killing, burning and forced movement that followed the fall of cities; it does not turn conquest into pure romance.

The Nile Unified: Writing, Pyramids and the Bureaucratic State

Around 3200 BCE a set of signs appeared in the Nile Valley; by about 2900 BCE they could be read phonetically. Tradition assigns the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt to Menes, while modern research identifies him incompletely with Narmer, Aha and other early kings. Djoser then built the Step Pyramid at Saqqara, Khufu compressed national logistics into a mountain at Giza, Ahmose drove the Hyksos from the Delta and opened the New Kingdom, and the Ebers Papyrus copied experience, spell and anatomy into one scroll. The six chapters tell one story: how Egypt used writing, flood, labour and kingship to turn a river into a durable state machine.

The Oceanic Turn: From Cape of Good Hope to Circumnavigation

In 1488 Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope; in 1492 Columbus crossed the Atlantic to the Caribbean; in 1494 the Treaty of Tordesillas pre-divided the “New World”; in 1498 da Gama reached Calicut; in 1500 Cabral reached Brazil; in 1519 Magellan’s fleet set out to circle the globe. Together these are not one “discovery” but a series of voyages, royal licences, capital and violence.

The Rise of the Ottomans: From Frontier Beylik to Empire

Traditional chronology treats 1299 as the starting point of the Ottoman state; in 1326 Orhan took Bursa, in 1354 the Ottomans crossed the Dardanelles, in 1396 they crushed an international crusade at Nicopolis, in 1453 they captured Constantinople, and in 1522 they took Rhodes. The six chapters are not isolated victories but the accumulation of four capacities: frontier manpower, fiscal capital, imperial legitimacy, and control of the straits and sea.

Phoenicia and Carthage: Harbours, Letters and Hannibal

Around the 10th century BCE, merchants from Tyre, Sidon and Byblos carried harbours, trading posts and alphabetic writing onto the sea. They built no continental empire, yet stretched a maritime network from the Levant to North Africa, Spain and Sicily. Tradition assigns Carthage's foundation in 814 BCE to the Tyrian princess Dido; the archaeological layers are about a century later. In the 5th century BCE Hanno sailed down the West African coast; in 218 BCE Hannibal crossed the Alps and brought war into Italy; in 216 BCE his double envelopment at Cannae nearly destroyed a large Roman army. The six chapters tell one story: how a network run through nodes lost the strategic initiative to Rome's system of mobilisation.

The Roman Empire: Frontier, Tax and Legitimacy

In 9 CE the Teutoburg Forest stopped Roman expansion east of the Rhine; in 80 the Colosseum opened; in 96 Domitian was assassinated and Nerva adopted Trajan; in 212 Caracalla granted citizenship to the empire's free inhabitants; after 235 the third-century crisis exposed army, tax and succession problems at once; in 313 the Milan agreement proclaimed religious toleration; in 410 Alaric sacked Rome. The seven chapters are not isolated rise-and-fall nodes but four accounts of one empire: frontier, finance, succession and legitimacy.

The Roman Republic: Law, Legions and Dictatorship

In 509 BCE the Romans expelled their king and replaced royal power with two annually elected consuls; in 451–450 the Twelve Tables turned rules into public text; in 264–241 the First Punic War pulled the Republic into the Mediterranean; in 202 Scipio defeated Hannibal at Zama; around 107 Marius began recruiting the landless; in 44 Caesar was assassinated; in 27 Octavian concentrated real power as Augustus and princeps. The seven chapters are not seven great men but four structures: magistracies, public law, war finance and army loyalty.

Sumer: From Temple Cities to the Akkadian Empire

Around 4500 BCE Eridu in southern Mesopotamia became one of the earliest cities, its temple built on stacked platforms. Around 4100 BCE counting tokens and pictographs appeared; around 4000 BCE Uruk became the largest city of Mesopotamia and a temple economy and bureaucracy took shape; by about 3700 BCE irrigation-supported city-states had emerged. The long urbanisation of 3500–3000 BCE left proto-cuneiform tablets from Uruk IV with about two thousand distinct signs; by 3200 BCE writing had become a systematic tool of economic management, and by 3000 BCE cuneiform had matured. Around 2700 BCE epic tradition made Gilgamesh king of Uruk. In 2334 BCE Sargon seized power, founded the Akkadian Empire, defeated Lugalzagesi and unified the Sumerian city-states. This story uses ten nodes to trace how cities, writing and kingship generated one another, and how the independence of the city-state ended before an empire.

Sun Quan: A Successor's Vassalage and Imperial Claim

This person-centered line opens with Sun Ce's rise in Jiangdong in 195 and ends with Sun Quan's death in Jianye in 252, in eight chapters. It first sets out the foundation Sun Ce laid by pacifying Jiangdong, then follows Sun Quan succeeding after the assassination, stabilizing the situation, moving the capital to Moling and building Stone City. Facing Liu Bei's invasion, he declared vassalage to Cao Pi and accepted the title King of Wu, yet refused to send a hostage and built his own bureaucracy, before proclaiming himself emperor at Wuchang, sending a fleet to Yizhou, and dying in his later years. The line shows how this successor weighed alliance and vassalage again and again to secure Jiangdong and gradually earn his own legitimacy.

Gold Empires of West Africa: Ghana, Mali and Songhai

Around the fifth century CE the Ghana Empire rose in the Sahel between the Senegal and Niger rivers; it controlled the trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt and by the ninth century was one of the richest polities in West Africa. In the thirteenth century Mali replaced Ghana; in 1312 Mansa Musa carried gold to Cairo on his pilgrimage to Mecca; in 1352 Ibn Battuta reached the Malian court; in the fifteenth century Songhai expanded from Gao; in 1591 a Moroccan army destroyed Songhai. Together these are not “Africa without history” but a long interaction of rivers, desert routes, Islamic scholarship and imperial governance.

The Yuan Dynasty: From Steppe Khan to Emperor of China

In 1251 Möngke was proclaimed great khan by the Altai River; two years later Kublai campaigned against Dali, and in 1258 Hulagu took Baghdad. In 1260 Kublai and his brother Ariq Böke each claimed the throne, and the empire fell into civil war. Kublai won with the resources of the Han lands, moved his capital to Dadu and changed the dynastic name to Yuan. Marco Polo reached Shangdu in 1275; Lin'an surrendered in 1276; Wen Tianxiang was executed at Dadu in 1279; in 1280 the Yuan completed its unification and promulgated the Shoushi calendar. Kublai died in 1294. This story uses twelve nodes to trace the birth of the Yuan, including the destruction wrought by conquest and the resistance of the conquered; it does not present "unification" as a single-line victory.

Zheng He's Seven Voyages: Treasure Ships, Monsoons and Tribute

In 1405 a Ming fleet sailed beyond Chinese waters through Champa, Malacca, Java and Ceylon to Calicut on the Malabar Coast. For nearly three decades Zheng He sailed six more times, reaching East Africa; the seventh voyage left in 1430, and after the fleet returned the state did not continue the series. The Ming History records 62 large ships and 27,800 soldiers and sailors on the first voyage; later accounts often inflate the total number of vessels, and this story keeps the original wording separate from later summaries.

Zhuge Liang: From the Longzhong Plan to the Wu Zhang Plains

This person-centered line runs from Zhuge Liang's Longzhong Plan and joining Liu Bei in 207 to his death in camp at the Wu Zhang Plains in 234, in eight chapters. It follows Zhuge Liang's own stages: setting the three-way division route for Liu Bei at their first meeting, negotiating the Sun–Liu alliance in Jiangdong, opening his office as chancellor after Liu Bei's death, sending Deng Zhi to repair the Shu–Wu alliance, campaigning south to pacify Nanzhong, presenting the Chu Shi Biao and advancing to Hanzhong, withdrawing from the first northern expedition after the loss at Jieting, and dying during the standoff of the fifth expedition. The line shows how the survival of the weak state of Shu was staked on a stable alliance, internal order, and the rhythm of the northern expeditions.

Cao Cao: From Chancellor of Jinan to King of Wei

Story

Local administration, incorporated troops, a base of power, then the Duchy and Kingdom of Wei

This person-centered line runs from Cao Cao's appointment as Chancellor of Jinan in 187 to his enfeoffment as King of Wei in 216, in eight chapters. It does not retell the campaigns of Guandu or Red Cliffs; it follows Cao Cao's own stages: early local administration, incorporating the Qingzhou Yellow Turbans as armed force, holding Yan Province as a base, advancing on Xuzhou, campaigning against Zhang Xiu, eliminating Lü Bu, and finally turning his power into a fief with its own ancestral temple and bureaucracy through the Duke and King of Wei steps. The line shows how a mobile war faction took root in court legitimacy, grain and manpower, and institutional personnel.

From local administrator to King of Wei, Cao Cao rose by institutionalizing court legitimacy, grain and manpower, and personnel, turning a mobile war faction into the bureaucratic Duchy of Wei.

The story, chapter by chapter

Each chapter opens the underlying event
  1. 01187 CE

    Cao Cao appointed Chancellor of Jinan

    Cao Cao was appointed Chancellor of Jinan by Emperor Ling. He vigorously reformed the administration, dismissing eight corrupt county magistrates and banning improper sacrifices, bringing order to Jinan. However, because he offended local strongmen and eunuchs, Cao Cao was soon transferred.

    Opening: early local administration shows his reforming hand.

    In 187, Cao Cao was appointed Chancellor of Jinan by Emperor Ling of Han. The event record says he vigorously reformed the administration in office, dismissing eight corrupt county magistrates and banning improper sacrifices, which brought order to the commandery.

    This chapter belongs to his early career as a local official. The record shows his methods began with removing corrupt magistrates and regulating ritual and sacrifice, a pattern later echoed in how he managed personnel within his own faction.

    His measures offended local strongmen and eunuchs, and he was soon transferred away from Jinan. The record does not detail his next posting; what is certain is that this local administration episode stands as an early record of his governance, not yet the start of a separatist regime.

  2. 02191 CE

    Incorporating the Qingzhou Yellow Turbans

    Cao Cao defeated a 300,000-strong Qingzhou Yellow Turban army in Yan Province and incorporated their elite into his own forces, called the 'Qingzhou Corps'. This force became Cao Cao's foundation; he established himself in Yan Province and began his quest for control of the Central Plains.

    Armed origins: absorbing rebel troops into the Qingzhou Corps.

    In 191, Cao Cao fought the Qingzhou Yellow Turbans in Yan Province. The event record says he defeated a 300,000-strong Qingzhou Yellow Turban force and incorporated its elite troops, who became known as the Qingzhou Corps.

    These absorbed troops became the foundation of Cao Cao's armed strength. The record states he established himself in Yan Province on this basis and began to contend for the Central Plains. Compared with the later regular military administration, his manpower at this stage still came mainly from absorbed rebel forces; the record does not detail his grain supply or territorial holdings.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter marks the armed starting point of Cao Cao's shift from local official to warlord: the Qingzhou Corps was both his manpower and the basis for expanding his base.

  3. 03192 CE

    Defeats remaining Yellow Turbans and holds Yan Province

    In Yan Province, Cao Cao defeated the remaining Yellow Turbans, incorporating 300,000 surrendering troops, greatly increasing his strength. Local elites recommended Cao Cao as Governor of Yan Province. Cao Cao began to build his own base, laying the foundation for his eventual unification of the north.

    Building a base: recommended Governor of Yan Province, with territory and title.

    In 192, Cao Cao routed the remaining Yellow Turbans in Yan Province. The event record says he accepted the surrender of 300,000 men, greatly increasing his strength, and local elites recommended him as Governor of Yan Province.

    Holding the title of Governor of Yan Province meant Cao Cao had his own territory and a commandery-level office. The record notes he began to build his own base, laying the groundwork for his eventual unification of the north. The record does not expand on the household registers, grain stores, or staff of the province; those details are not preserved.

    This chapter follows the absorption of troops in the previous one: with the Qingzhou Corps in hand, Cao Cao anchored his mobile army in a fixed province through Yan Province.

  4. 04193 CE

    Campaign against Xuzhou

    To avenge his father Cao Song (killed by subordinates of Tao Qian, Governor of Xuzhou), Cao Cao led an army to attack Xuzhou, slaughtering hundreds of thousands of civilians along the way. It was said that the Si River was blocked by corpses. Cao Cao's atrocities caused widespread outrage; Chen Gong, Zhang Miao, and others rebelled against Cao Cao in Yan Province, allying with Lü Bu.

    Expansion and cost: eastward for his father's death, triggering a rear revolt.

    In 193, Cao Cao led an army against Xuzhou. The event record says the cause was that his father Cao Song had been killed by subordinates of Tao Qian, Governor of Xuzhou, and Cao Cao marched to avenge him.

    The record states that Cao's army slaughtered hundreds of thousands of civilians along the route, leaving the saying that the Si River was blocked by corpses. The atrocities drew widespread outrage; Chen Gong, Zhang Miao, and others then rebelled against Cao Cao in Yan Province, allying with Lü Bu.

    In the person-centered line, the eastward advance on Xuzhou was an expansion that came at the cost of an unstable rear. The Yan Province revolt left Cao Cao caught between two fronts before his base was secure; the record does not give detailed casualty figures or marching dates.

  5. 05197 CE

    Campaign against Zhang Xiu

    Cao Cao led an army against Zhang Xiu, who surrendered. However, Cao Cao then took Zhang Xiu's aunt as a concubine. Zhang Xiu, enraged, suddenly rebelled and attacked Cao Cao's camp. Cao Cao's eldest son Cao Ang, his nephew Cao Anmin, and his general Dian Wei were killed. Cao Cao barely escaped with his life. This was a costly defeat for Cao Cao.

    A setback: surrender then revolt, costing his son, nephew, and a general.

    In 197, Cao Cao campaigned against Zhang Xiu. The event record says Zhang Xiu first surrendered, but Cao Cao then took Zhang Xiu's aunt as a concubine. Resentful, Zhang Xiu suddenly rebelled and raided Cao Cao's camp.

    The surprise attack broke Cao Cao's camp formation. The record states that Cao Cao's eldest son Cao Ang, his nephew Cao Anmin, and the general Dian Wei were killed, and Cao Cao barely escaped with his life. This was a costly defeat; the record does not detail troop strengths or casualty numbers.

    In the person-centered line, this is a chapter of setback during Cao Cao's expansion. The reversal after surrender shows that the allegiance of local armed groups was not yet stable, and Cao Cao's treatment of those who submitted directly affected the outcome.

  6. 06198 CE

    Eliminating Lü Bu

    Cao Cao led his army to besiege Xiapi (present-day Suining, Jiangsu) and captured and executed Lü Bu and his generals Chen Gong and Gao Shun. Lü Bu, known for his treachery, ultimately brought about his own end. Cao Cao accepted the surrender of Zhang Liao and others, incorporating Xuzhou into his domains.

    Securing the eastern front: besieging Xiapi and absorbing Xuzhou.

    In 198, Cao Cao besieged Xiapi, in the area of present-day Suining, Jiangsu. The event record says he captured and executed Lü Bu and his generals Chen Gong and Gao Shun.

    The record describes Lü Bu as treacherous and ultimately self-destroyed. Cao Cao accepted the surrender of Zhang Liao and others, and Xuzhou was incorporated into his domains. This chapter removed Lü Bu's force from the eastern front, leaving no direct separatist ruler to contain Cao Cao in the Yan and Xuzhou direction.

    The record does not detail the later appointments of Lü Bu's surrendered generals. In the person-centered line, eliminating Lü Bu cleared Cao Cao's eastern front before Guandu, leaving him room to concentrate against Yuan Shao in Hebei.

  7. 07213 CE

    Enfeoffed Duke of Wei

    Under pressure from Cao Cao, Emperor Xian of Han issued an edict appointing Cao Cao as Duke of Wei, granting him the Nine Bestowals, and establishing a Wei bureaucracy. Ten commanderies of Jizhou were given as his fief, with the capital at Yecheng.     Institutional turning point: the Duchy of Wei separates from Han bureaucracy.

    In 213, under pressure from Cao Cao, Emperor Xian of Han issued an edict appointing Cao Cao Duke of Wei, granting him the Nine Bestowals, establishing a Wei bureaucracy, and giving him ten Jizhou commanderies as fief with the capital at Yecheng.

    The event record notes this was the first time in the Eastern Han that a man not of the imperial clan was made a duke; the only earlier case was Wang Mang as Duke of Anhan. The Duchy of Wei signified that Cao Cao had formally separated from the Han bureaucracy, with his own ancestral temple, secretariat, and officials, taking a decisive step toward replacing the Han.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter is an institutional turning point: before it, Cao Cao issued orders through Han offices; after it, he held a fief with its own system. The record does not detail the administration of the ten commanderies or the roster of officials.

    Power capped: rank above all kings, Cao Pi named crown prince of Wei.

    In 216, under pressure, Emperor Xian appointed Cao Cao King of Wei, his status higher than all other feudal kings, with imperial regalia. The event record lists a twelve-pendant crown, a gold root-carriage chariot, a six-horse team, and five-colored secondary chariots.

    Cao Cao established his royal capital at Yecheng, set up an ancestral temple, and named Cao Pi crown prince of Wei. The record states that although he still nominally called himself a Han minister, he was de facto emperor, and the Eastern Han existed in name only.

    This chapter caps Cao Cao's path to power: from Chancellor of Jinan to Duke and King of Wei, the fief institutions were completed step by step. The record does not describe the later arrangements for replacing the Han; the succession under Cao Pi is covered in related stories.

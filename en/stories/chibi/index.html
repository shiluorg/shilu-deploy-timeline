Red Cliffs: The Blocker on the Yangtze

Story

After Jingzhou falls, Cao Cao moves south; the Sun–Liu alliance halts his momentum by river war and marriage

In 208 Cao Cao campaigned south against Jingzhou; Liu Cong surrendered the province, and after Liu Bei was routed at Changban he withdrew to Xiakou and joined Eastern Wu. The Sun–Liu alliance stopped Cao's forces with a fire attack at Red Cliffs, then sealed the alliance by marriage, contested Jiangling, and took the southern commanderies. After Zhou Yu's early death Cao Cao turned to Guanzhong, while Sun and Liu held the Yangtze line in long stalemate. This story follows seven event nodes to show how river warfare and alliance politics held Cao Cao's unifying momentum to the north bank; read the chapters in chronological order.

After Jingzhou surrenders, Cao Cao presses south; the Sun–Liu alliance holds by river warfare and marriage, and Red Cliffs blocks unification, leaving north and south in stalemate.

    Jingzhou falls without a pitched battle; Cao gains ships and a navy, and the southern front shifts at once.

    In 208 Cao Cao led a large army south against Jingzhou. The Jingzhou governor Liu Biao happened to die of illness, and his son Liu Cong succeeded him. Urged by Kuai Yue and Fu Xun, Liu Cong surrendered the whole province to Cao Cao.

    Liu Bei, who had been lodged in Jingzhou, pulled back from Fancheng but was routed at Changban near Dangyang by Cao Chun's Tiger and Leopard Cavalry. Only the arrival of Guan Yu's naval force saved him, and he fled to Xiakou. Cao Cao then occupied Jiangling, gaining a large fleet and a naval force.

    In this chapter Cao Cao takes Jingzhou almost without a pitched battle and gains the means to fight downstream by river. For Sun Quan and Liu Bei the defensive line has been pushed to the Yangtze, and holding back this momentum depends on whether the two can form an alliance.

    The fire attack breaks the chained fleet; the plan to unify the south goes bankrupt here.

    Cao Cao led about 200,000 men downstream along the Yangtze. The Sun–Liu allied force of about 50,000, commanded by Zhou Yu, Cheng Pu, and Zhuge Liang, met him at Red Cliffs (northwest of present-day Chibi, Hubei). Cao's troops were unaccustomed to naval warfare and chained their ships together to reduce rolling.

    Zhou Yu's subordinate Huang Gai feigned surrender and, taking advantage of a southeast wind, steered fire ships laden with kindling and oil into Cao's naval camp. The fire spread, destroying the chained fleet and reaching the shore camps. Cao Cao burned his remaining ships and retreated through Huarong Road; in the mud his losses were more than half his force.

    The battle shattered Cao's plan to unify the south. Liu Bei seized the four southern commanderies of Jingzhou, Sun Quan consolidated Jiangdong, and the Three Kingdoms pattern began to take shape. The decisive ground was the river: the northern army's land advantage could not be deployed afloat.

    The marriage turns military cooperation into a political arrangement, later planting the Jingzhou dispute.

    After Red Cliffs, to strengthen the Sun–Liu alliance, Sun Quan followed Zhou Yu's advice and gave his younger sister Lady Sun in marriage to Liu Bei. It was a political marriage, not a union based on personal inclination between the two sides.

    According to the event record, Lady Sun was brave and fond of martial ways; over a hundred maidservants attended her, all carrying swords, so that Liu Bei felt afraid every time he entered her chambers. The marriage kept Sun–Liu cooperation alive for the time being, with the two sides aligned against Cao Cao.

    Yet this arrangement, which bound interests to a family tie, was not stable. It steadied the alliance after the war, but it also planted the later dispute over Jingzhou: the alliance was a military necessity, yet it always mixed in calculations of territory and strength.

    The Jiangling siege and the southern four commanderies fix the postwar territory on both sides.

    After Red Cliffs, Zhou Yu led tens of thousands of troops to besiege Jiangling (present-day Jingzhou, Hubei), defended by Cao Ren. After a year of fierce fighting, Cao Ren broke out and retreated, and Zhou Yu seized Jiangling.

    Meanwhile Liu Bei took the opportunity to move south and recover the four southern commanderies of Jingzhou: Wuling, Changsha, Guiyang, and Lingling. He proclaimed himself Governor of Jingzhou and set his seat at Gong'an. Sun Quan agreed to lend him part of the Nan commandery so that the two could jointly oppose Cao Cao.

    This chapter fixes the actual controlled areas after the war: Wu holds the Jiangling line, Liu Bei establishes himself in the southern four commanderies, and the land loan ties the two sides' interests together for the moment. Jingzhou thus becomes the most sensitive border zone within the alliance.

  5. 05210 CE

    Zhou Yu's early death ends Wu's western expansion; the alliance enters the post-Zhou Yu phase.

    Zhou Yu had proposed to Sun Quan a campaign to conquer Yizhou, but on his way back to Jiangling, at Baqiu (present-day Yueyang, Hunan), his old wounds opened and he died in camp, aged thirty-six.

    The event record describes him as a master of music and a magnanimous, refined man; at Red Cliffs he commanded the Sun–Liu alliance to a great victory over Cao Cao and was a key figure in the founding of Eastern Wu. In his final letter he recommended Lu Su as his successor. Sun Quan deeply mourned the loss, regarding him as a fallen chief aide.

    Zhou Yu's death interrupted Wu's plan to expand west into Yizhou. The alliance then entered the post-Zhou Yu phase, with defence and diplomacy carried on by the Lu Su line; Sun–Liu cooperation and friction both continued to shift within that adjustment.

    The ability-only hiring policy supplies Cao Wei with talent from lower backgrounds.

    In 210 Cao Cao issued his Call for Talent at Yecheng, openly proposing the principle of employment based solely on ability. The edict uses Duke Huan of Qi as an example: one cannot rely only on men of flawless conduct. Those with stains on their reputation, laughable conduct, or even lacking benevolence and filial piety, but with the skills to govern and command armies, should still be recommended.

    This policy broke the Eastern Han path of selecting officials by moral reputation and the ethical code. Many people of low birth but real ability entered the Cao Wei administration. The hiring standard shifted from family standing and fame to practical competence, supplying Cao Wei with executors at every level.

    Alongside the military stalemate, this was Cao Wei's internal adjustment of talent: as its northern controlled area grew, who would govern localities and lead armies became as important as the fighting itself.

    Cao Cao pacifies Guanzhong in the west, opening a western front alongside the southern stalemate.

    After his defeat at Red Cliffs, Cao Cao turned west to campaign against Ma Chao and Han Sui in Guanzhong. Ma Chao allied with Han Sui and ten other Guanzhong warlords, fielding a total of 100,000 men and holding Tong Pass.

    Cao Cao employed Jia Xu's rift stratagem: in a letter to Han Sui he deliberately blotched and altered passages, so that Ma Chao suspected Han Sui of colluding with Cao Cao. When the alliance fell into internal disorder, Cao Cao launched a general attack at Weinan. Ma Chao was badly defeated and fled to Liangzhou; Han Sui was beaten back to Huangzhong, and Guanzhong was pacified by Cao Cao.

    While the southern line held in stalemate, Cao Cao removed the Guanzhong warlord threat in the west. With the north and Guanzhong consolidated, his pressure on the south did not disappear; the front merely shifted west for the time being, and the Sun–Liu alliance still faced this long-term opponent.

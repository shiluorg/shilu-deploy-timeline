The Kushans: Gold, Gandhara and Silk Road Buddhism

Story

37–143: how a Central Asian empire put Greece, India and Buddhism on one coin

In the first century CE the Kushans rose from the Yuezhi confederation; in 37 Kujula Kadphises took the title of king; around 60 Vima Kadphises issued gold coinage, linking the empire’s trade and money to Rome, India and Central Asia; in 78 Kanishka I ascended and the empire reached its height; in 102 Gandharan Buddhist art peaked; around 110 Buddhist art entered China through the Western Regions; in 143 Kushan culture and art reached their height. Together these show a “crossroads empire” joining civilisations through gold, cities and Buddhist art.

Kushan power lay less in a large contiguous territory than in control of key passages: from the Indian Ocean over the Hindu Kush into Central Asia and onto the Silk Roads. Gold coins were instruments of commercial credit, Gandharan art mixed Hellenistic form with Indian Buddhist content, and Buddhism’s eastward spread depended on monks, translations and trade routes, not a single decree. The Kushans turned difference into circulating signs.

    Around AD 37, Kujula Kadphises, chief of the Kushan tribe (one of the five Yuezhi Xihou), united the other four tribes, established the Kushan Empire in the Bactria region, and formally proclaimed himself 'King of Kings', establishing his capital at Bactra (modern Balkh, Afghanistan).     Kujula’s royal title marked the Kushans’ move from one clan in the Yuezhi confederation to a polity with a formal kingship. Title, coin and city were the minimal toolkit for turning a clan coalition into a state.

  2. 0260 CE

    Kushan King Vima Kadphises Minted Gold Coins

    During the reign of the Kushan Empire's King Vima Kadphises (c. AD 60-80), large-scale minting of gold and copper coins began. His gold coins featured a Hellenistic design, with the king's portrait on the obverse and Indian deities like Shiva on the reverse. The weight standard of Vima Kadphises's gold coins (about 8 grams) matched that of Roman gold coins, facilitating international trade across the Silk Road.

    Vima’s gold coinage shows the Kushans entering a transregional credit system. Gold was not a collector’s item but a tool for settling caravans, cities and taxes on one scale.

  3. 0378 CE

    Kanishka I of the Kushan Ascended the Throne

    In AD 78, the most famous king of the Kushan Empire, Kanishka I, ascended the throne. He ruled during the golden age of the Kushan Empire, establishing his capital at Purushapura (modern Peshawar, Pakistan) and strongly supporting Buddhism. Kanishka built magnificent stupas and monasteries and presided over the Fourth Buddhist Council. The Kushan Empire reached its zenith under Kanishka's rule.

    Under the patronage of Emperor Kanishka I of the Kushan Empire, Buddhist art in the Gandhara region (present-day northern Pakistan and eastern Afghanistan) entered a golden age. Artisans blended Hellenistic realism with Indian religious symbolism to create the first anthropomorphic statues of the Buddha and Bodhisattvas. Representative works include a colossal standing Buddha about 15 meters tall, draped in a Greek-style robe with distinctly European facial features. Gandharan art later spread to China along the Silk Road, profoundly influencing the cave temple sculptures at Yungang and Longmen.

    Gandharan art used Hellenistic proportions, drapery and faces to tell Buddhist stories. It was not “Greek art plus Buddhism” but a recombination in specific workshops, patronage and markets.

    Under Kanishka I, Kushan Buddhist art reached its zenith. Numerous Buddha and Bodhisattva statues and sutras were transmitted via the southern Silk Road to Western Regions kingdoms such as Khotan, Kashgar, and Kucha. Artisans there imitated Gandharan styles, carving the first cave temples (e.g., Kizil Caves) and painting Buddhist murals with Hellenistic features. This Buddhist art later provided models for the Mogao Caves and Yungang Grottoes, marking a significant starting point for the Sinification of Buddhism.

    Buddhist art entered China through the Western Regions via monks, caravans and oasis cities. A religious route was also a route of translation, patronage and pilgrimage.

    Under Kanishka I and his successors, the Kushan Empire entered its golden age. Its capital, Peshawar (present-day Pakistan), became a Buddhist center, with the Kanishka Stupa, about 200 meters high, one of the tallest structures in the world at that time.

    Under Kanishka I and his successors, the Kushan Empire entered its golden age. Its capital, Peshawar (present-day Pakistan), became a Buddhist center, with the Kanishka Stupa, about 200 meters high, one of the tallest structures in the world at that time. Gandharan art, blending Greek, Persian, and Indian styles, produced numerous Buddha statues and narrative reliefs. The Kushans also controlled the central Silk Road trade, enjoyed economic prosperity, and cultural diversity, becoming one of the four great empires of the world at the time (along with Rome, Parthia, and Han China).

    The cultural height of 143 was the result of long accumulation and a signal of dispersal. In an empire held together by many nodes, a weak centre lets the edges go their own way.

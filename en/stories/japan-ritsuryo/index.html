Japan's Ritsuryo State: Buddhism, Prince Shotoku and Nara

Story

552–784: from clan coalition to a code-based bureaucracy

In 552 King Seong of Baekje sent Buddhist scriptures, an image, and liturgical ornaments to the Japanese court; 538 is also cited. Prince Shōtoku became regent in 593 and later instituted the twelve court ranks; in 604 he issued the Seventeen-Article Constitution. After the 645 coup, the reform rescript declared land and people public; the Taihō code was compiled in 701; in 710 the capital moved to Heijō-kyō, in 741 the kokubunji system was established, and in 752 the Great Buddha of Tōdai-ji was dedicated.

Japan's ritsuryō state was not a copy of Tang China. Buddhism supplied a sacred language for kingship, legal codes supplied formats for taxation and appointment, and the capital supplied a spatial order of power; but clans, local notables and succession still operated inside the system. Centralisation was more a continuous negotiation between centre and locality.

  1. 01552 CE

    Buddhism enters Japan: court diplomacy and clan rivalry

    King Seong of Baekje sent Buddhist scriptures, an image, and liturgical ornaments to the Japanese court; 552 is the traditional year of official introduction, while 538 is also cited. Buddhism spread first among the elite and through clan competition, then took root through art and ritual.

    In 552 King Seong of Baekje sent Buddhist scriptures, an image, and liturgical ornaments to the Japanese court; 538 is also cited. Buddhism was first resisted by Shintō priests and aristocrats, then took root through monasteries, ceremonies, and continental art.

    552 is the date traditionally given for the official introduction of Buddhism into Japan: King Seong of Baekje sent Buddhist scriptures, an image, and liturgical ornaments to the Japanese court; 538 is also cited and the two years coexist.

    Buddhism was first resisted by Shintō priests and aristocrats, then took root through monasteries, ceremonies, and the appeal of continental art. The introduction was not a single nationwide conversion; it was the product of court diplomacy, clan competition, and the attraction of Buddhist art.

  2. 02593 CE

    Shōtoku's regency: the twelve ranks and the beginning of a bureaucracy

    In 593 Shōtoku became crown prince and regent after his aunt Empress Suiko came to the throne, and he held that position until his death in 622. During the regency he instituted twelve court ranks marked by cap colour, opening an institutional path toward appointment by merit.

    In 593 Shōtoku became crown prince and regent after his aunt Empress Suiko came to the throne, and held the position until his death in 622. During the regency he instituted the twelve court ranks marked by cap colour; Hōryū-ji was built in 607.

    In 593, after his aunt Empress Suiko came to the throne, Shōtoku became crown prince and regent, and he remained in that position until his death in 622. He resumed the interrupted envoys to Sui China and brought continental craftsmen and clerks into Japan.

    During the regency he instituted the twelve court ranks: each was marked by the colour of the cap, and rank no longer depended solely on uji birth. That did not abolish the clans, but it opened an institutional path toward appointment by merit; Hōryū-ji was built in 607 and became a representative of Asuka Buddhism and wooden architecture.

  3. 03604 CE

    The Seventeen-Article Constitution: a moral charge to officials

    In 604 Shōtoku issued the Seventeen-Article Constitution. It was not a modern written constitution but a code of conduct for the ruling class, upholding a unified sovereign, appointment by merit, official responsibility, and obedience from subjects.

    In 604 Shōtoku issued the Seventeen-Article Constitution. It was not a modern written constitution but a code of conduct for the ruling class: a unified sovereign, appointment by merit, and official responsibility. Most provisions took effect later, and authorship is disputed.

    In 604 Shōtoku issued the Seventeen-Article Constitution. It was not a modern written constitution but a code of conduct for the ruling class: it upheld a unified sovereign, appointment by merit, official responsibility toward the governed, and obedience from subjects.

    The articles also urged adherence to the Buddhist treasures—Buddha, dharma, and saṃgha—and forbade local officials from collecting taxes or exacting corvée on their own. Most provisions took effect only much later; some historians also doubt whether the extant text is wholly Shōtoku's work. To read it as a modern constitution is to misunderstand Asuka politics.

  4. 04645 CE

    The Taika reforms: writing land and people as "public"

    In 645 Prince Nakano Ōe and Nakatomi Kamatari launched a coup and destroyed the Soga clan; the following year's rescript proclaimed that land and people belonged to the public, and set out central organs, a census, and a poll tax. The reforms copied Tang models, though many measures took effect only decades later.

    In 645 Prince Nakano Ōe and Nakatomi Kamatari launched a coup and destroyed the Soga clan; the following year's rescript declared land and people public and set out central organs, a census, and a poll tax. The reforms copied Tang models, though many measures took effect only decades later.

    In 645 Prince Nakano Ōe and Nakatomi Kamatari launched a coup and destroyed the Soga clan. In the first month of the following year the court issued the reform rescript in four articles: land and people were no longer private property but belonged to the "public," that is, the emperor; administrative and military organs answerable to the emperor were to be set up in the capital and provinces; a census was to be taken and land distributed fairly; and a new, more equitable tax system was to be created.

    The reforms also copied Tang institutions, codified laws, and began a nationwide road network. Many measures later attributed to "Taika" may have taken effect only decades afterwards. But the phrase "land and people belong to the public" became the fulcrum for the continuing law-state programme.

  5. 05701 CE

    The Taihō code: Tang institutions become Japanese law

    The Taihō code was compiled in 701 as the administrative and penal code of the early Nara period, modelled on Tang codes. It turned central organs, the kuni–gun–ri hierarchy, and local appointments into executable articles.

    The Taihō code was compiled in 701 as the administrative and penal code of the early Nara period, modelled on Tang codes and in force until the late 8th century. The original is lost; its content survives largely in the Yōrō code of 718.

    The Taihō code was compiled in 701 as the administrative and penal code of the early Nara period, modelled on Tang codes and in force until the late 8th century. It turned central government organs, the kuni–gun–ri hierarchy, and the appointment of local officials into executable articles.

    The code included administrative ryō and penal ritsu. The original is lost; its content survives largely in the Yōrō code of 718. It gave Japan a common bureaucratic language, but the practical power of local gentry did not disappear.

  6. 06710 CE

    Heijō-kyō and Tōdai-ji: Buddhism inside the state machine

    In 710 the capital moved to Heijō-kyō (Nara), the first permanent capital, laid out like Tang Chang'an. In 741 the kokubunji system was established, in 743 Tōdai-ji and its Great Buddha were initiated, and in 752 the image was dedicated.

    In 710 the capital moved to Heijō-kyō (Nara), the first permanent capital, laid out like Tang Chang'an. In 741 the kokubunji system was established, in 743 Tōdai-ji and its Great Buddha were initiated, and in 752 the image was dedicated.

    In 710 the capital was moved to Heijō-kyō (Nara). It was the first permanent capital, laid out on the model of Tang Chang'an; the Nara period lasted until 784, when Sinicization and Buddhism both reached a high point.

    In 741 Emperor Shōmu established a monastery and a nunnery in each province, tying local temples to a Nara-centred system; in 743 he initiated Tōdai-ji and its Vairocana Great Buddha. Construction of the Great Buddha Hall began in 745, dedication was held in 752, and the seated figure stands nearly 15 metres high. The Nara central government had a Council of State and ministries, plus an Office of Deities; the sovereign took the title tennō and stood both at the apex of the bureaucracy and at the centre of Shintō ceremony. Religion was not a private matter; it was part of the state project.

Israel and Judah: Temple, Schism and Exile

Story

1000–732 BCE: how small kingdoms in the shadow of empires left a text

Around 1000 BCE David is presented in traditional narrative as the king who united the twelve tribes and made Jerusalem his capital; in 975 Solomon began the First Temple; it was completed in 966; in 958 the Queen of Sheba visited Solomon; in 931 the kingdom split into Israel and Judah after Solomon’s death; in 732 Assyria captured Samaria and the northern kingdom fell. Together these are not a complete “biblical history” but the story of small kingdoms using temple, kingship and text to build memory between the empires of Egypt, Assyria and Babylon.

The biblical text is a composite of memory, theology and political claim, not a modern archive. The scale of the “united monarchy” of David and Solomon does not fully match archaeological and textual evidence; the centralisation of the Temple was both religious reform and a means of royal taxation and sacrificial monopoly. After the northern kingdom fell, Judah wrote the “lost brothers” into its own history.

    After defeating the house of Saul, David united the twelve tribes of Israel, captured the Jebusite city of Jebus, renamed it Jerusalem and made it the capital. He brought the Ark of the Covenant to Jerusalem, making the city the religious and political center of Judaism. David reigned for about 40 years (c. 1010–970 BCE), establishing a united kingdom of Israel from the Euphrates to the Egyptian border through a series of military conquests, laying the foundation for the prosperity of the Solomonic era.

    David made Jerusalem the centre of kingship and sacrifice. The claim of uniting twelve tribes comes from later texts; archaeology can confirm that Jerusalem gradually became a regional political node.

  2. 02975 BCE

    King Solomon begins construction of the First Temple

    King Solomon began building the Temple on Mount Moriah in Jerusalem (traditionally where David offered sacrifice to Yahweh). The project used cedar wood and gold from Tyre and took seven years to complete. The Temple became the sole religious center of the Jewish people, housing the Ark of the Covenant and the Ten Commandments, and consolidating the identity of the twelve tribes of Israel.

  3. 03966 BCE

    Solomon's Temple completed, Ark brought in

    After seven years, the First Temple was completed. King Solomon presided over a grand dedication ceremony, moving the Ark of the Covenant from the City of David to the Temple's inner sanctuary. The priests sacrificed 120,000 cattle and sheep, and the entire nation celebrated for fourteen days. The Temple became the spiritual symbol of Israel and the pilgrimage center for the three annual festivals.

  4. 04958 BCE

    Queen of Sheba visits Solomon

    According to the Bible, the Queen of Sheba (possibly from modern Yemen or Ethiopia), having heard of Solomon's wisdom, came to Jerusalem with a caravan of camels laden with spices, gold, and precious stones to test him. Solomon answered all her questions, and they exchanged gifts and established trade agreements.

    After Solomon's death, his son Rehoboam succeeded. The northern tribes, led by Jeroboam, demanded a reduction in forced labor. Rehoboam refused and sent an overseer to suppress them. The ten northern tribes then proclaimed Jeroboam as king, forming the Kingdom of Israel (capital Shechem), while the southern tribes of Judah and Benjamin remained with Rehoboam, forming the Kingdom of Judah (capital Jerusalem). The united monarchy of Israel thus split.

    After Solomon’s death the northern tribes rejected Jerusalem’s centralisation, and the kingdom split into Israel and Judah. The division was not simply religious apostasy but also taxation, labour service and local elite interests.

  6. 06732 BCE

    Assyria Captures Samaria; Kingdom of Israel Falls

    The Assyrian army finally captured Samaria. The Assyrian king deported the inhabitants of Israel to various parts of the Assyrian Empire, creating the 'Lost Ten Tribes'. Israelite territory was turned into an Assyrian province, and foreign immigrants were settled there, forming the later Samaritans. The kingdom of Israel was completely destroyed.

    Assyria captured Samaria and the northern kingdom fell. Much of the population was deported and remaining inhabitants mixed with incoming groups; Judah interpreted the disaster as the consequence of apostasy and wrote it into later texts.

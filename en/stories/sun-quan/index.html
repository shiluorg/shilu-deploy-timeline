Sun Quan: A Successor's Vassalage and Imperial Claim

Story

Inheriting his brother's cause, trading alliance and vassalage for Jiangdong's security and legitimacy

This person-centered line opens with Sun Ce's rise in Jiangdong in 195 and ends with Sun Quan's death in Jianye in 252, in eight chapters. It first sets out the foundation Sun Ce laid by pacifying Jiangdong, then follows Sun Quan succeeding after the assassination, stabilizing the situation, moving the capital to Moling and building Stone City. Facing Liu Bei's invasion, he declared vassalage to Cao Pi and accepted the title King of Wu, yet refused to send a hostage and built his own bureaucracy, before proclaiming himself emperor at Wuchang, sending a fleet to Yizhou, and dying in his later years. The line shows how this successor weighed alliance and vassalage again and again to secure Jiangdong and gradually earn his own legitimacy.

Inheriting his brother's cause, Sun Quan traded alliance and vassalage alternately for Jiangdong's security and legitimacy, until he proclaimed himself emperor.

  1. 01195 CE

    Sun Ce emerges in Jiangdong

    Sun Ce, using the Imperial Seal his father Sun Jian had recovered as collateral, borrowed 3,000 troops from Yuan Shu, crossed the Yangtze, and defeated Liu Yao, Yan Baihu, and others, occupying Kuaiji and Wu commanderies.     The family foundation: Sun Ce pacifies Jiangdong and lays Wu's groundwork.

    In 195, Sun Ce used the Imperial Seal his father Sun Jian had recovered as collateral to borrow three thousand troops from Yuan Shu, then crossed the Yangtze downstream. The event record says he defeated Liu Yao, Yan Baihu, and others, occupying Kuaiji and Wu commanderies.

    The record states Sun Ce knew how to use people and kept strict military discipline; many heroes of Jiangdong rallied to him. This campaign laid the foundation for his younger brother Sun Quan's later establishment of the state of Wu.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter is the background of Sun Quan's inheritance: Jiangdong's territory and followers were not won by Sun Quan himself but were the starting point left by his brother Sun Ce. The record does not detail the organization of Sun Ce's force or the attachment of each commandery.

  2. 02200 CE

    Sun Ce assassinated, Sun Quan succeeds

    While hunting at Dantu Mountain, Sun Ce was fatally wounded by assassins sent by the family of Xu Gong, whom he had killed. He was only 26. On his deathbed, Sun Ce entrusted his younger brother Sun Quan to Zhang Zhao and Zhou Yu, saying: 'In external matters, consult Zhou Yu; in internal matters, consult Zhang Zhao.' Sun Quan succeeded, and under the guidance of Zhang Zhao and Zhou Yu, stabilized Jiangdong, gradually…

    The succession: taking over young, stabilized by Zhang Zhao and Zhou Yu.

    In 200, while hunting at Dantu Mountain, Sun Ce was fatally wounded by assassins sent by the retainers of Xu Gong; he died of his wounds at the age of twenty-six. The event record says that on his deathbed he entrusted his younger brother Sun Quan to Zhang Zhao and Zhou Yu, saying that external matters should be referred to Zhou Yu and internal matters to Zhang Zhao.

    After succeeding, Sun Quan, guided by Zhang Zhao and Zhou Yu, stabilized Jiangdong and gradually developed into a power that stood alongside Cao Cao and Liu Bei.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter opens Sun Quan's succession: taking over his brother's territory and followers at a young age, he relied on senior aides to hold the situation together. The record does not detail the handover of power or internal reactions at his accession.

  3. 03212 CE

Thereafter Sun Quan renamed Moling as Jianye, meaning 'establishing imperial enterprise.' Jianye became…

    Building the base: moving west to Moling, building the city, naming it Jianye.

    In 212, following the advice of Zhang Hong, Zhuge Liang, and others, Sun Quan moved his administrative center from Jingkou, modern Zhenjiang, west to Moling, modern Nanjing. The event record says he built Stone City on Qingliang Mountain, using the natural topography.

    The city relied on the Yangtze for defense, held a strategic position, and housed the navy and arms depots. Sun Quan then renamed Moling as Jianye, meaning establishing imperial enterprise. The record states Jianye became the political and economic center of Eastern Wu and marked the beginning of Nanjing as the capital of six dynasties.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter builds Sun Quan's Jiangdong base: after moving west, Jiangdong defense and strongpoints had a fixed anchor. The record does not detail the scale of Stone City's construction or its garrison.

  4. 04221 CE

    Receives Wei's title King of Wu

    Liu Bei's invasion of Wu forced Sun Quan to avoid a two‑front war. Sun Quan sent Lu Xun and Zhuge Jin to maintain good relations with Wei while secretly building up his strength. Although Cao Pi had hoped to take advantage of the situation to destroy Wu, he hesitated…

    Vassalage for time: pressed on two fronts, declaring vassalage to Cao Pi.

    As Liu Bei invaded Wu, Sun Quan declared himself a vassal of Cao Pi to avoid a two-front war and accepted the title King of Wu from Wei. The event record says Cao Pi granted him the Nine Bestowals and appointed him Governor of Jingzhou.

    Sun Quan sent Lu Xun, Zhuge Jin, and others to maintain good relations with Wei while secretly building up his strength. The record states that although Cao Pi had hoped to take advantage of the situation to destroy Wu, he hesitated after being advised by Liu Ye and did not act.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter shows Sun Quan buying time and security through vassalage: between Shu and Wei, he briefly accepted a tributary standing to avoid pressure from two sides. The record does not detail the bilateral exchanges after the enfeoffment or Wu's internal war preparations.

  5. 05222 CE

    Refuses hostage, adopts era Huangwu

    After his defeat at Xiaoting, Sun Quan, while nominally submissive to Wei, refused to send his son Sun Deng as a hostage to Luoyang. Cao Pi demanded the hostage several times, but was refused, and Wei–Wu relations deteriorated. Sun Quan changed his reign name to Huangwu (indicating that he was no longer using Wei's era name), established his own bureaucracy, and in effect became an independent state.

    Keeping autonomy: no hostage, own bureaucracy and era name.

    After the defeat at Xiaoting, Sun Quan, while nominally submissive to Wei, refused to send his son Sun Deng as a hostage to Luoyang. The event record says Cao Pi demanded the hostage several times, was refused each time, and Wei–Wu relations deteriorated.

    Sun Quan changed his era name to Huangwu, indicating he no longer used Wei's era name, and established his own bureaucracy, in effect becoming an independent state.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter preserves Sun Quan's autonomy: vassalage was nominal, and he did not trade his crown prince for Wei's trust. Instead, he drew on an independent system through a new era name and his own officials. The record does not detail the official structure set up under Huangwu.

  6. 06229 CE

    Legitimacy completed: dynasty Wu, era Huanglong, division agreed with Shu.

    In 229, urged on by his ministers, Sun Quan formally ascended the imperial throne at Wuchang, modern Ezhou, Hubei, proclaiming the dynasty name Wu and changing the era name to Huanglong. The event record says he posthumously honored his father Sun Jian as Emperor Wulie of Wu and his elder brother Sun Ce as Prince Huan of Changsha; he appointed Lu Xun Senior General-in-Chief and Zhuge Jin General-in-Chief.

    After Sun Quan proclaimed himself emperor, Shu Han sent Chen Zhen to congratulate him. The two sides agreed that after destroying Wei they would divide the empire equally: the territory west of Hangu Pass to Shu, and east of the pass to Wu.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter completes Sun Quan's legitimacy: from King of Wu under Wei to self-proclaimed emperor, the Jiangdong regime now held its full title. The record does not detail the enfeoffments after accession or the precise terms of the Shu alliance.

  7. 07230 CE

    Maritime expansion: a ten-thousand-man fleet sails out, leaving an early contact record.

    In 230, hoping to find the legendary islands of Yizhou, modern Taiwan, and Danzhou, possibly Japan, Sun Quan sent generals Wei Wen and Zhuge Zhi with ten thousand armed men in large ships. The event record says the fleet reached Yizhou and spent a year there, making contact with the local inhabitants.

    The record states more than eight or nine out of ten soldiers died of disease, so the fleet could not stay long. This was one of the earliest large-scale official naval expeditions in Chinese history and an early recorded instance of significant contact between mainland China and Taiwan.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter shows Sun Quan opening a maritime direction beyond Jiangdong. The record does not detail the fleet's route, the schedule of its voyage, or observations of the island.

    Closing: the longest-reigning Wu ruler dies, a young son succeeds.

    In 252, Sun Quan died of illness in Jianye, with the posthumous name Great Emperor, buried at Jiangling Mausoleum. The event record says he was the longest-lived of the three monarchs of the Three Kingdoms.

    During his reign, he inherited his brother Sun Ce's foundations in Jiangdong, crushed Cao Cao at Red Cliffs, defeated Liu Bei at Xiaoting, established Eastern Wu, and expanded into Jiaozhou, modern Guangdong, Guangxi, and northern Vietnam. After his death, his son Sun Liang succeeded at age ten, with Zhuge Ke and Sun Jun acting as regents.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter closes Sun Quan's story: from succeeding his brother, to imperial claim and expansion, to a young son succeeding under ministerial regency. The record does not detail his late court arrangements or the politics after his death.

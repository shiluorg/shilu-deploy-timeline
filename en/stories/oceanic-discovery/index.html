The Oceanic Turn: From Cape of Good Hope to Circumnavigation

Story

1488–1522: how Europeans joined the Atlantic and Indian Oceans into one route

In 1488 Dias rounded the Cape of Good Hope; in 1492 Columbus crossed the Atlantic to the Caribbean; in 1494 the Treaty of Tordesillas pre-divided the “New World”; in 1498 da Gama reached Calicut; in 1500 Cabral reached Brazil; in 1519 Magellan’s fleet set out to circle the globe. Together these are not one “discovery” but a series of voyages, royal licences, capital and violence.

The “Age of Discovery” was not a sudden increase in European courage but the alignment of navigation, interstate rivalry, commercial capital and religious mission. It forced the Indian Ocean network long maintained by Arab, Indian, Southeast Asian and African merchants into Iberian royal routes; it also dragged existing societies in the Americas, Africa and Asia into conquest, disease and colonisation. For the “discovered,” the story was often a different one.

    Dias sailed south along West Africa with two caravels and, near thirty degrees south, was driven off the coast by a storm for some two weeks; turning east and finding no land, he realised he had passed the tip of the continent and ran north to what is now Mossel Bay. The voyage proved the Atlantic and Indian oceans connected, opening the way for Vasco da Gama.

    Rounding the Cape solved how to enter the Indian Ocean from the Atlantic; it did not solve whom to trade with once there. Opening a route and building commerce are two different things.

    On April 17, 1492, Queen Isabella I of Spain signed the Capitulations of Santa Fe with the Genoese navigator Christopher Columbus, funding his westward voyage to find a route to India and China. On August 3, Columbus set sail from Palos with three ships: the Santa María, the Pinta, and the Niña. On the morning of October 12, lookout Rodrigo de Triana sighted land on an island in the Bahamas (San Salvador). Columbus believed he had reached the East Indies and called the natives "Indians." He brought back gold, spices, and captives, opening sustained European contact with the Americas.

    Columbus did not arrive in an empty land. The Caribbean already held Taíno, Kalinago and other societies; the European “discovery” narrative rewrote those people and their land as resources available for possession.

  3. 031494 CE

    Treaty of Tordesillas signed

    On June 7, 1494, Spain and Portugal, mediated by the papacy, signed the Treaty of Tordesillas, fixing the line of demarcation 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands (about 46° W longitude). The treaty granted Portugal all lands east and Spain all lands west, essentially dividing the non-European world into two colonial empires. Brazil later became Portuguese because of this treaty.

  6. 061519 CE

    Magellan's Fleet Begins Circumnavigation

    On 10 August 1519, Portuguese navigator Ferdinand Magellan set sail from Seville with five ships and about 270 men, heading west to find a westward route to the Spice Islands (Maluku Islands). This was the first circumnavigation of the Earth.

Zhuge Liang: From the Longzhong Plan to the Wu Zhang Plains

Story

Alliance, internal governance, and the rhythm of the northern expeditions, the thread through Shu's chancellorship

This person-centered line runs from Zhuge Liang's Longzhong Plan and joining Liu Bei in 207 to his death in camp at the Wu Zhang Plains in 234, in eight chapters. It follows Zhuge Liang's own stages: setting the three-way division route for Liu Bei at their first meeting, negotiating the Sun–Liu alliance in Jiangdong, opening his office as chancellor after Liu Bei's death, sending Deng Zhi to repair the Shu–Wu alliance, campaigning south to pacify Nanzhong, presenting the Chu Shi Biao and advancing to Hanzhong, withdrawing from the first northern expedition after the loss at Jieting, and dying during the standoff of the fifth expedition. The line shows how the survival of the weak state of Shu was staked on a stable alliance, internal order, and the rhythm of the northern expeditions.

Working from the Longzhong Plan, Zhuge Liang staked the survival of a weak state on alliance, internal governance, and the rhythm of the northern expeditions, until his death in camp.

    The start: setting the division route and becoming Liu Bei's strategist.

    In 207, Liu Bei visited Zhuge Liang three times at his thatched cottage in Longzhong. The event record says Zhuge Liang laid out his famous Longzhong Plan: occupy Jingzhou and Yizhou, ally with Eastern Wu, and when the time was right, launch two campaigns from Jingzhou and Hanzhong to conquer the north.

    This strategic blueprint gave Liu Bei's faction a clear political direction. Liu Bei persuaded Zhuge Liang to leave his retreat and appointed him Army Advisor General; Zhuge Liang became Liu Bei's most important strategist.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter opens Zhuge Liang's career: before leading troops, he entered Liu Bei's faction with a long-term strategic framework. How each step of the plan was later carried out is unfolded in the event records that follow.

    A diplomatic mission: persuading Sun Quan at Chaisang to ally against Cao Cao.

    In 208, Cao Cao's forces threatened Jingzhou and Liu Bei fled to Xiakou. The event record says Zhuge Liang volunteered to go with Lu Su to meet Sun Quan at Chaisang.

    In Sun Quan's court, Zhuge Liang convinced him to ally with Liu Bei against Cao Cao, pointing out that Cao's army was weary from its long march, unfamiliar with naval warfare, and had an unstable rear. Sun Quan decided to ally with Liu Bei and ordered Zhou Yu and Cheng Pu to lead their elite troops to join Liu Bei's force.

    In the person-centered line, this is the first time Zhuge Liang shaped a larger situation as a diplomatic envoy: the Longzhong Plan's line of allying with Eastern Wu was carried out through his own mission. The record does not detail the precise arguments in the meeting or the exact troop dispositions.

    Heading the state: under a young ruler, opening the chancellor's office in charge.

    After Liu Bei's death, his son Liu Shan ascended the throne, changing the era name to Jianxing. The event record says Zhuge Liang, as Chancellor and Minister over the Masses, opened his office and effectively held all power.

    Once in charge, Zhuge Liang set about rebuilding relations with Eastern Wu, sending Deng Zhi as envoy to Wu. Seeing that Zhuge Liang was in charge, Sun Quan reciprocated by sending Zhang Wen to Shu, and the two sides agreed to attack Wei together. The record states the Shu–Wu alliance was restored, laying the foundation for later northern expeditions and Wei–Wu wars.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter turns Zhuge Liang from strategist into head of government: under a young ruler, the chancellor's office took charge of Shu affairs. The record does not detail the staff of the office or the internal reforms that followed.

    Securing the rear: restoring Shu–Wu friendship to free the eastern flank.

    After Liu Bei's death, relations between Wu and Shu Han were strained. The event record says Zhuge Liang, assessing the situation, sent the minister Deng Zhi to Jianye to reaffirm the Shu–Wu alliance.

    Deng Zhi pointed out to Sun Quan that Shu and Wu were as interdependent as lips and teeth, and that only by uniting could they oppose Wei. Sun Quan was convinced, broke off his vassal relationship with Wei, and re-established the alliance with Shu. The two sides agreed to divide the Wei empire equally after its destruction. This diplomatic success created a stable rear area for Zhuge Liang's northern expeditions.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter focuses on the repair of the alliance: the previous chapter opened the office and took charge; this one freed the eastern flank. The record does not detail the round trip of Deng Zhi's mission or the precise terms of the Wu–Shu covenant.

    Calming the rear: winning hearts to end revolt and draw Nanzhong's resources north.

    In 225, rebellions broke out in Shu Han's Nanzhong region, modern Yunnan, Guizhou, and southern Sichuan, led by the local strongmen Yong Kai, Zhu Bao, Gao Ding, and Meng Huo. The event record says Zhuge Liang personally led a three-pronged southern campaign, adopting a strategy of winning hearts first.

    Around Lake Dian, Zhuge Liang defeated Meng Huo and captured and released him seven times, until Meng Huo was sincerely convinced and said the southerners would never rebel again. Zhuge Liang appointed local able men to office in Nanzhong and drew the region's gold, silver, cattle, horses, and war elephants into support for the northern expeditions, strengthening Shu Han.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter calms the rear before the northern expeditions: Nanzhong was pacified first, then the campaign north could be considered. The record does not detail the march routes or the long-term effects of local governance.

    Expedition begins: memorial of resolve, encamping in Hanzhong for the moment.

    After pacifying the south, Zhuge Liang felt the time was ripe for a northern expedition. The event record says he presented the Chu Shi Biao to Liu Shan, expressing his determination to conquer the north, drive out the traitors, and restore the Han.

    He then led his army to Hanzhong, encamped at Mianyang, modern Mian County, Shaanxi, drilled his troops, repaired equipment, and waited for the right moment to launch the expedition. This marked the beginning of Zhuge Liang's northern expeditions for Shu Han.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter starts the expeditions: with the rear settled and the alliance restored, Shu's military and political center moved north to Hanzhong. The record does not detail the defensive deployments or grain arrangements after the advance.

    The closest attempt: three commanderies respond, withdrawn after Jieting.

    In 228, Zhuge Liang feinted an attack via the Xie Valley route but actually led his main force out of Mount Qi, modern Li County, Gansu. The event record says Nan'an, Tianshui, and Anding commanderies rebelled and responded to him, throwing the Guanzhong region into turmoil.

    Emperor Ming of Wei personally traveled to Chang'an to take command and sent Zhang He to the rescue. Zhuge Liang ordered Ma Su to hold Jieting, modern Zhuanglang, Gansu. Ma Su disobeyed orders by camping on a hill; Zhang He cut off his water supply, and the Shu army broke up in disorder. Zhuge Liang executed Ma Su and withdrew all his forces to Hanzhong; the first northern expedition failed in its closest approach.

    In the person-centered line, this was the expedition closest to its goal: the response of three commanderies was lost through the loss at Jieting. The record does not detail the local terrain of the Jieting battle or the schedule of the withdrawal.

    The end: dying of overwork in the standoff, withdrawing and entrusting affairs.

    In 234, during his fifth northern expedition, Zhuge Liang faced Sima Yi at the Wu Zhang Plains, modern Qishan, Shaanxi, for more than a hundred days. The event record says he implemented military-agricultural colonies to prepare for a long war, but overwork led to illness and he died in camp.

    On his deathbed, he ordered Yang Yi, Jiang Wei, and others to keep the death secret and withdraw to Shu. Zhuge Liang's death was a great loss to Shu Han, and his northern expedition plans were laid aside. Liu Shan posthumously honored him as Marquis Zhongwu; later generations respectfully call him Marquis Wu.

    In the person-centered line, this chapter closes the story: from the Longzhong Plan to the Wu Zhang Plains, alliance, internal governance, and the rhythm of the expeditions run through it all. The record does not detail the handover of power in Shu after the withdrawal.

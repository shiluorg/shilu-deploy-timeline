Hanzhong: How the Triangle Solidified into Tripartite Rule

Story

Hanzhong, Fancheng, and Jiangling move at once; unification turns into a three-way balance

From 217 Cao Cao and Liu Bei stalemated along the Qinling line; after Xiahou Yuan's death at Dingjun Mountain Liu Bei held Hanzhong, and Guan Yu then flooded seven armies at Fancheng, shaking the Central Plains. Lü Meng crossed in merchant guise to seize Jiangling; Guan Yu fell, and Jingzhou passed to Wu. Liu Bei then became King of Hanzhong, Cao Cao died at Luoyang, and Cao Pi founded Wei. This story follows seven event nodes to show how the Hanzhong, Jingzhou, and Central Plains lines intertwined, turning the earlier momentum toward unification into a three-way balance of Wei, Shu, and Wu; read the chapters in chronological order.

Dingjun Mountain, the Fancheng flood, and the seizure of Jingzhou form one triangular chain; Liu Bei's kingship and Cao Pi's Wei turn unification into a three-way balance.

    The Hanzhong front opens; the gateway to Yizhou becomes a must-contest ground for Cao and Liu.

    After Liu Bei was secure in Yizhou, Fa Zheng advised him to seize Hanzhong. Liu Bei personally led his army to attack Hanzhong, first sending Zhang Fei, Ma Chao, and Wu Lan against Xiabian (present-day Cheng County, Gansu), but this detachment was defeated by Cao Hong.

    Liu Bei's main force stationed at Yangping Pass, facing Xiahou Yuan and Zhang He. Cao Cao then led a large army to reinforce, and the two sides were locked in stalemate for months, each winning and losing some encounters. Because grain transport was difficult, Cao Cao finally withdrew in 219, leaving Cao Hong and Cao Zhen to defend the area.

    This chapter opens the Hanzhong standoff. Hanzhong was the gateway to Yizhou, and whoever held it controlled the initiative northward and eastward. Neither side could swallow the other in the opening phase, and the contest settled into a long stalemate.

  2. 02218 CE

    Mount Dingjun and the Han River

    Killing the commander at Dingjun Mountain and raiding the grain drive Cao off; Liu Bei takes all Hanzhong.

    Liu Bei, with Fa Zheng and Huang Zhong, confronted Xiahou Yuan's Cao force at Mount Dingjun (present-day Mian County, Shaanxi). Fa Zheng devised a plan: Liu Bei set fire to the Cao palisades at night; Xiahou Yuan came with troops to the rescue, and Huang Zhong, from a high position, launched a surprise attack and killed Xiahou Yuan in the melee.

    The Cao army promoted Zhang He as commander, and Cao Cao himself led a large force to reinforce. Liu Bei held the strategic points and refused to give battle, while sending Zhao Yun and Huang Zhong to raid Cao Cao's grain supply. With his grain nearly exhausted, Cao Cao withdrew in 219, and Liu Bei occupied all of Hanzhong.

    The Dingjun Mountain engagement removed the Cao commander in Hanzhong, and after the grain line was raided Cao Cao was forced to abandon this theatre. Liu Bei thus secured Yizhou's northern gateway and drew a new boundary with Cao Wei along the Qinling line.

  3. 03219 CE

    Guan Yu's northern campaign shocks the Central Plains, while the alliance rift widens behind him.

    After Liu Bei occupied Hanzhong, Guan Yu set out from Jiangling on the northern campaign, besieging Cao Ren at Fancheng (north of present-day Xiangyang, Hubei). Cao Cao sent Yu Jin and Pang De with about 30,000 men and seven armies to relieve the siege.

    In August the Han River flooded. Guan Yu used large warships to attack the Cao camps and flooded the seven armies. Yu Jin surrendered, and Pang De was executed. Guan Yu then besieged Xiangyang, and Cao Cao even considered moving the capital to avoid him. Many counties in the area responded to Guan Yu; this was the peak of his military career.

    The northern campaign shook the Central Plains, but the weakness of his rear also stood exposed. The Sun–Liu alliance still held on the surface, yet the unresolved dispute over Jingzhou remained: the stronger the forward push, the more the river defense behind him depended on Eastern Wu's attitude.

    Jiangling is raided and Guan Yu falls; Jingzhou goes to Wu, and the Sun–Liu alliance breaks.

    Eastern Wu commander Lü Meng took advantage of Guan Yu's northern campaign and the weakness in his rear. Disguising his force as merchant ships, he hid his elite soldiers below deck and sailed west day and night, capturing Jiangling and Gong'an by surprise. Mi Fang and Shi Ren surrendered.

    When Guan Yu heard the news he turned back, sending envoys to Lü Meng again and again. Lü Meng treated the families of Guan Yu's soldiers well, destroying their morale; Guan Yu's men gradually deserted, and he retreated to Maicheng. Sun Quan tried to persuade him to surrender but failed, then sent Zhu Ran and Pan Zhang to cut off his escape. Guan Yu and his son Guan Ping broke out and reached Linju (present-day Yuan'an, Hubei), where they were captured by Ma Zhong, a subordinate of Pan Zhang, and executed. Sun Quan sent Guan Yu's head to Cao Cao at Luoyang; Cao Cao buried the head with noble rites, while Sun Quan buried his body at Dangyang.

    In this chapter Jingzhou passed to Eastern Wu, and the Sun–Liu alliance broke formally. The Fancheng offensive collapsed with it; Liu Bei lost the vital Jingzhou base, and the balance among the three powers was rewritten.

    With the kingship and royal office, Liu Bei's regime takes formal shape.

    In 219, urged on by his ministers, Liu Bei erected an altar at Mianyang (present-day Mian County, Shaanxi) and declared himself King of Hanzhong, continuing the Han imperial line as a member of the Han clan.

    He made Xu Jing Grand Tutor, Fa Zheng Minister of the Interior, Guan Yu General of the Front, Zhang Fei General of the Right, Ma Chao General of the Left, and Huang Zhong General of the Rear. Liu Bei's authority in Shu was further consolidated, and his military and economic strength stood in a tripartite balance with Cao Wei and Eastern Wu.

    The kingship marks the formation of Liu Bei's regime: holding Yizhou and Hanzhong, he was no longer merely a regional warlord but set up a royal office and appointed officials under a princely title. This arrangement also laid the institutional groundwork for his later imperial founding.

    Cao Cao's death hands over Cao Wei power; the old order reaches its closing stage.

    In 220 Cao Cao fell seriously ill at Luoyang. Before dying he issued a Final Command requesting burial on a hill west of Yecheng, in ordinary clothes, with no mausoleum, no mound, no trees, and no precious objects in the tomb. He also gave instructions for his concubines, telling them to sell perfume and make shoes to support themselves.

    Cao Cao was sixty-six, with the posthumous title of King Wu. His death marked the end of an era, and his son Cao Pi quickly completed the transition from Han to Wei.

    This chapter concerns the succession of power rather than a new campaign. The military and political resources built up in the north over years passed to Cao Pi, and the nominal existence of the Han court entered its final closing stage with Cao Cao's death.

    The Han abdicates and Wei is founded; the Three Kingdoms pattern is fully established.

    Cao Cao's son Cao Pi, using political maneuvers, forced Emperor Xian of Han, Liu Xie, to abdicate and ascended the throne at Luoyang, proclaiming his dynasty Wei and changing the era name to Huangchu. The Eastern Han, restored by Emperor Guangwu, came to an end after 195 years. Cao Pi granted Liu Xie the title of Duke of Shanyang, with a fief of 10,000 households and generous treatment.

    The following year Liu Bei proclaimed himself emperor at Chengdu, naming his dynasty Han (known to history as Shu Han) and claiming to continue the Han line. In 221 Sun Quan accepted Wei's enfeoffment as King of Wu, and in 229 he officially declared himself emperor. The Three Kingdoms tripartite equilibrium was fully established.

    In the end, decades of warlord infighting closed into three coexisting regimes. The contests over Hanzhong, Jingzhou, and Fancheng in the preceding chapters form the geographic and military background of this settlement; the unification drive was fixed into three boundaries, and a new phase of confrontation began.

Gold Empires of West Africa: Ghana, Mali and Songhai

Story

500–1591: how trans-Saharan trade sustained empires and cities

Around the fifth century CE the Ghana Empire rose in the Sahel between the Senegal and Niger rivers; it controlled the trans-Saharan trade in gold and salt and by the ninth century was one of the richest polities in West Africa. In the thirteenth century Mali replaced Ghana; in 1312 Mansa Musa carried gold to Cairo on his pilgrimage to Mecca; in 1352 Ibn Battuta reached the Malian court; in the fifteenth century Songhai expanded from Gao; in 1591 a Moroccan army destroyed Songhai. Together these are not “Africa without history” but a long interaction of rivers, desert routes, Islamic scholarship and imperial governance.

West African imperial wealth came less from the legend of “streets paved with gold” than from control of route nodes: whoever could move gold north and salt south could tax, pay soldiers and build cities. Mali and Songhai converted trade revenue into Islamic scholarship, mosques and court institutions, but succession and frontier governance remained fragile; the defeat of Songhai by Morocco in 1591 did not end West African history.

    Approximately 500–1200 CE, the Ghana Empire (located in present-day Mauritania and western Mali) became the first empire in West Africa. Its king was called 'Ghana' (war leader). The empire amassed immense wealth by controlling the West African gold trade and trans-Saharan trade routes. The capital, Koumbi Saleh, was divided into a merchants' city and a royal city, with a population of about 20,000. Arab travelers noted that 'gold was weighed with a scale.' Ghana declined after being conquered by the Almoravids around 1076.

    Ghana’s “empire” was more a coalition controlling routes and clients than a modern centralised state. Its power came from location: desert caravans to the north and gold-producing regions to the south.

    In 805, the king of the Ghana Empire (Ghana meaning 'war lord') tightened control over the southern gold-producing regions, decreeing that all gold nuggets be submitted to the treasury and only gold dust be used among the people. The gold trade brought enormous tax revenue to Ghana, and its capital Kumbi Saleh became the terminal of trans-Saharan trade routes, where camel caravans brought salt and textiles in exchange for gold.

    The key to the gold trade was not the mine but the ability to move gold from producing areas to route nodes. Transport, taxation and caravan protection were each harder than “owning a mine.”

    In 1235, the Mandinka leader Sundiata Keita defeated King Sumanguru of Sosso at the Battle of Kirina and established the Mali Empire. Mali controlled the West African gold and salt trades, with its capital at Niani becoming a center of learning.

    In 1235, the Mandinka leader Sundiata Keita defeated King Sumanguru of Sosso at the Battle of Kirina and established the Mali Empire. Mali controlled the West African gold and salt trades, with its capital at Niani becoming a center of learning. The empire reached its zenith under Mansa Musa (1312-1337); during his pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324 he distributed so much gold in Cairo that the Egyptian gold price dropped for a decade. The Sankore University in Timbuktu became one of the world's most famous Islamic centers of learning.

    Mali replaced Ghana and extended West African networks south to the upper Niger. An empire’s durability depended on keeping different routes and peoples within one order.

    In 1464, Sonni Ali became king of the Songhai Empire and began massive military expansion. He built a powerful river fleet (Niger River flotilla) and captured Timbuktu and Djenné.

    In 1464, Sonni Ali became king of the Songhai Empire and began massive military expansion. He built a powerful river fleet (Niger River flotilla) and captured Timbuktu and Djenné. In 1493, Muhammad Ture (Askia Muhammad) seized the throne, perfected the administrative system, and promoted Islam. The Songhai Empire became the largest empire in West African history, stretching from the Atlantic to central Niger. It collapsed after being defeated by Moroccan musketeers in 1591.

    Songhai expanded from Gao and made the Niger bend the imperial core. It inherited Mali’s trade and scholarly networks and also its fragile frontiers and succession problems.

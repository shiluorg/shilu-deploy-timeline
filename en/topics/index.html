Architecture and art

Architecture and art are not a ranking of masterpieces. Read each site through four questions: who paid, who built, who looked, and how it was changed later. Construction, completion, decoration, enlargement and restoration often belong to different centuries; the stone, columns and paintings visible today may have been rebuilt, moved or patched. Start with Caral's platforms and plazas in the Americas, then the pyramids of the Nile, the Acropolis of the Greek poleis, and Hagia Sophia in Byzantium. Materials, labour and budgets also shaped what was possible. Keep 'built' separate from 'what survives now'. List changes of use under different regimes, and label the evidence behind any reconstruction.

The emergence of cities

Cities did not follow one straight line from village to metropolis. Walls, temples, markets, water supply, waste disposal and cemeteries did not appear in the same order everywhere. Uruk in Mesopotamia and Mohenjo-daro and Harappa in the Indus valley are often used to prove a 'urban revolution', but their populations, power structures and economies differed. Separate four measures: site size, density, public buildings and written records. Then ask how irrigation, trade and craft production sustained them. Maintenance of channels, stores and walls often explains more than a single founding event. Do not judge one city by another's standard, or mistake an archaeologist's reconstruction for a street view. Map houses, workshops, stores and cemeteries together; urban-rural relations often explain more than walls. Who maintained canals and granaries also set how long a city could last.

Law and institutions

Law and institutions are easily distorted by applying modern words to ancient texts. A 'code' is not a modern civil code, a 'citizen' is not a universal voter, and 'autocracy' does not mean one person can do anything. Ask who could take part in judgment, who enforced it, who bore the consequences, and how far the text was from practice. From the Hammurabi stele to Solon's reforms in the Greek poleis, the Twelve Tables of the Roman Republic and Justinian's code in Byzantium, writing rules down changed power relations, while local custom and strongmen could still override the page. Ancient codes often mix cases, penalties and prices: they are rulebooks of rule, not declarations of rights. Institutions decide who is protected and who is excluded. Compare codes with contracts, judgments and local custom; literacy and appeal decide whom rules actually protect.

Medicine and health

Medicine and health is not a collection of ancient remedies for today. Separate three things: what the text says, what practitioners did, and what patients actually received. Egyptian papyri, Mesopotamian diagnostic records, Greek humoral theory, Baghdad's hospitals and translation movement, and Jenner's cowpox experiment represent knowledge, institutions, practice and public health at different levels. Ancient medicine contained observation as well as magic, hierarchy and commerce; mixing cure, comfort and power turns history into myth. Diet, nursing, sanitation and ability to pay often decided outcomes more than a recipe. Historical material is not a basis for self-diagnosis and cannot replace modern care. Read treatment records, instruments, materia medica and hospital accounts together; status, faith healing and public health often appear on the same page. Patients' accounts and nurses' work matter too.

Oceans and voyages

Oceans and voyages are not a hero's line of discoverers. Ports, monsoons, hulls, supplies, charts, tolls and violence all had to work together. Phoenician and Carthaginian harbours, Zheng He's treasure ships and tribute, and Vasco da Gama's passage round the Cape into the Indian Ocean rested on different fiscal and military structures. Monsoons set sailing seasons, ports set tax revenue, hulls set cargo, and supply points set range. Separate arrival, trade, colonization and diplomacy as four different contacts, then ask what crews, pilots, translators and local intermediaries did. Lines on a chart are clean; power at sea never is. Logs, port tolls, shipwrecks and charts show routes as temporary corridors of supply, wind, permission and force. Crew rations, disease and death rates measure a voyage as clearly as cargo lists.

Printing and reading

Printing and reading is not only a question of who invented printing first. Paper, ink, carvers, booksellers, readers and censorship all had to exist before a publishing trade could form. Woodblock, movable type, manuscript and printed book coexisted for centuries; the older form did not vanish when the newer appeared. The Diamond Sutra is often called one of the earliest surviving dated printed works, but that does not prove China invented printing first, nor that print produced 'knowledge democracy' overnight. Paper costs, the number of carvers and distribution networks explain why printing took off in one place more than a single invention. Separate technology, text, market and regulation, then follow a book from workshop to temple, academy and ordinary reader. Editions, prefaces, readers' notes and bans show how print was filtered; cheaper paper and book prices are part of reading history too.

Observing the world

Observing the world is not a parade of geniuses. Read it through questions, instruments, records and transmission. Babylonian astronomical records, Greek geometry, Baghdad's translation and algebra, and Copernicus's heliocentric model were forms of knowledge production under different institutions. Separate observation, calculation, instrument and theory, then ask who funded knowledge, in what language it was written, and where it was challenged. Whether records could be checked by later readers often says more about a tradition than a single flash of insight. Do not assume a new model was accepted at once, or that ancient observation equals modern science. The key is how error was recorded, corrected and passed on. Records, instrument error, translations and objections show whether a model was more accurate or merely more acceptable. Who paid for data and who preserved it matter as well.

Writing and knowledge

Writing and knowledge begins by separating writing, documents, libraries and schools. Mesopotamian cuneiform, Egyptian hieroglyphs, the Phoenician alphabet and Assyrian clay-tablet libraries solved different problems: accounting, ritual, administration and memory. Ask who wrote, what was written, for whom, and where it was kept; then trace how writing moved from a small scribal skill to an instrument of administration and education. Writing material, archival rules and literacy decide whose voices survive. Do not confuse 'having writing' with 'having literature', or 'having a library' with public access. Preservation has always involved selection, power and loss. Tablets, papyri, inscriptions and catalogues show what was copied, catalogued or deleted; survival is not importance. Scribal training, classification and access rules also shape what later readers can see.

THEMATIC JOURNEY

The emergence of cities

TopicTopics

Cities did not follow one straight line from village to metropolis. Walls, temples, markets, water supply, waste disposal and cemeteries did not appear in the same order everywhere. Uruk in Mesopotamia and Mohenjo-daro and Harappa in the Indus valley are often used to prove a 'urban revolution', but their populations, power structures and economies differed. Separate four measures: site size, density, public buildings and written records. Then ask how irrigation, trade and craft production sustained them. Maintenance of channels, stores and walls often explains more than a single founding event. Do not judge one city by another's standard, or mistake an archaeologist's reconstruction for a street view. Map houses, workshops, stores and cemeteries together; urban-rural relations often explain more than walls. Who maintained canals and granaries also set how long a city could last.

Suggested route: start with Uruk in Sumer, then Mohenjo-daro and Harappa in Indus and Vedic, and return to the Mesopotamian urban revolution and written administration. At each stop watch three things: water and farmland, public buildings and storage, waste and drainage. Walls do not always mean war, temples do not always mean theocracy, and grid streets do not always mean equality. Break 'city' into density, division of labour, power and networks before comparing regions, so that one place's experience is not turned into a universal law. This topic follows an event-first route: read urban events in Mesopotamia, the Indus valley and Mesoamerica, then use Sumer and Indus-Vedic as the two deepening stories; with only two stories available, no fourth is fabricated.

THEMATIC JOURNEY

Writing and knowledge

TopicTopics

Writing and knowledge begins by separating writing, documents, libraries and schools. Mesopotamian cuneiform, Egyptian hieroglyphs, the Phoenician alphabet and Assyrian clay-tablet libraries solved different problems: accounting, ritual, administration and memory. Ask who wrote, what was written, for whom, and where it was kept; then trace how writing moved from a small scribal skill to an instrument of administration and education. Writing material, archival rules and literacy decide whose voices survive. Do not confuse 'having writing' with 'having literature', or 'having a library' with public access. Preservation has always involved selection, power and loss. Tablets, papyri, inscriptions and catalogues show what was copied, catalogued or deleted; survival is not importance. Scribal training, classification and access rules also shape what later readers can see.

Suggested route: begin with cuneiform and schools in Sumer, hieroglyphs and scribes in The Nile Unified, the spread of the alphabet in Phoenicia and Carthage, and finish with the clay-tablet libraries of Assyria. At each stop ask what material was used, who the readers were, how texts were classified and stored, and who was excluded from literacy. Do not call an alphabet 'more advanced' because it is simpler, or a library 'public' because it exists. Writing history is also a history of selection and power; many texts vanished in fire, damp and war, and survival is itself a bias. This topic follows an event-first route: read events on writing, archives, books and education, then use the four existing stories for depth; stories and events index each other.

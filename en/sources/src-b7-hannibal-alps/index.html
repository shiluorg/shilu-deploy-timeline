Hannibal — The Alpine crossing

SourceSources
Hannibal — The Alpine crossing · Encyclopaedia Britannica (accessed 2026-11-25) Original source ↗

Uses departure from Cartagena, five months, descent on the 15th day, 25,000 infantry/6,000 cavalry and most of the original 37 elephants, candidate passes, the Second Punic War of 218–201 BCE, and the failure to march on Rome after Cannae; it does not say '60,000 troops'.

选段

Finally, on the 15th day, after a journey of five months from Cartagena, with 25,000 infantry, 6,000 cavalry, and most of his original 37 elephants, Hannibal descended into Italy.

The problem of determining the exact location of the camp is compounded by the fact that the name of the pass was either not known to Polybius and his sources or it was thought not sufficiently important to provide to mostly Roman readers. … Proposed routes have included the low passes at Montgenèvre, Little St. Bernard, and Mount Cenis, as well as the high passes at Col du Clapier–Savine Coche and Col de la Traversette.

Hannibal may have started from Cartagena with an army of around 90,000—including an estimated 12,000 cavalry—but he left at least 20,000 soldiers in Spain to protect his supply lines. In the Pyrenees his army, which included at least 37 elephants, met with stiff resistance from the Pyrenean tribes.

In 219 Hannibal attacked Saguntum, an independent Iberian city south of the Ebro River. … Thus began the Second Punic War, declared by Rome and conducted, on the Carthaginian side, almost entirely by Hannibal.

Hannibal, however, did not march on Rome but spent the winter of 216–215 in Capua, which declared its loyalty to Hannibal, possibly with the hope of being made Rome’s equal.

Hannibal was eventually called back to Africa in 203 BCE, where he was defeated by Scipio Africanus at the Battle of Zama in 202 BCE, ending the war. … Hannibal (born 247 BCE, North Africa—died c. 183–181 BCE, Libyssa, Bithynia [near Gebze, Turkey]).

Traditional chronology dates the Roman Republic to 509 BCE, when the Romans expelled their last king, Tarquinius Superbus, and replaced royal power with two annually elected consuls. The date comes from later historians, not from a day-by-day contemporary record.

The two consuls were primarily generals responsible for leading Rome's armies; in a military emergency that demanded unified command, the Senate and consuls could appoint a dictator in their place, but his supreme military command could not last longer than six months.

The Senate was in theory only an advisory body, yet in practice it wielded enormous power through the collective prestige of its members. The popular assemblies were divided between the centuriate assembly, which voted on war and peace and elected magistrates with military command, and the tribal assembly, which did most of the legislating and sat as a court for serious public offenses.

The Republic's key was therefore not 'rule by the people' but an oligarchic system that combined annual terms, paired magistrates, senatorial prestige and assembly votes. It prevented the return of kingship, but it did not close the power gap between patricians and plebeians.

Bank of England established: origin of modern central banking

The short version

London, 1694. There was no national bank building with columns and a bronze plaque. What appeared first was an act of Parliament and a group of people willing to lend the government money. The source states the occasion plainly: England was at war with France in the Low Countries and needed funds; the Bank of England was incorporated by act of Parliament in 1694 with the immediate purpose of raising money for that war.

The legacy entry prefers a bigger phrase: “the establishment of the modern central bank”. That is too large. It retrofits later functions onto 1694. Start with the narrower fact: the bank began as a chartered joint-stock bank, not as a born “bank of the state”.

Why it happened

War was expensive in the late seventeenth century. Tax revenue arrived slowly, military spending did not, and the government could not yet borrow in the flexible way later states would. The legacy entry says the bank lent £1.2 million to support the war with France and was allowed to issue banknotes in return. The source confirms the purpose — raising funds for the war against France — but does not, in that passage, itemise the £1.2 million or the note arrangement.

So the question in 1694 was not “how do we design a perfect central bank?” It was “how do we close this month's gap in the military budget?” Institutions often begin with a hole in a ledger, not with a blueprint.

What happened

The source says the Bank of England was incorporated by act of Parliament in 1694, allowed by royal charter to operate as a joint-stock bank with limited liability. That status matters: it was an institution funded by shareholders and authorised by a charter, not a department of government. It raised money for the state and also did business for itself; it dealt with the Treasury, but it was not the Treasury.

The legacy entry turns William Paterson into the founder of a personal enterprise. The source puts the weight on the act of Parliament and war finance. Paterson can appear as an early promoter, but a chartered bank should not be described as one merchant's private invention. Parliament's authorisation, shareholders' subscriptions and a large government loan were all needed to bring it into being.

What changed

One sentence in the source is decisive: during the nineteenth century the bank gradually assumed the responsibilities of a central bank. In 1694, it was not yet a central bank in the modern sense. It did not begin with a monopoly on note issue, did not begin as lender of last resort, and did not begin supervising every commercial bank. Those functions were added later, through financial crises, bank runs, government borrowing and the management of money.

The legacy claim that it was “the first true central bank” and that its “successful model was copied worldwide” can be read as later recognition, but not as a founding declaration. A more accurate description is that it was one of the earliest state banks to tie public credit to joint-stock capital for the long term; only later did it become the prototype of a central bank.

How to read the evidence

This entry carries one source, Britannica's article on the Bank of England. It directly supports three points: incorporation by act of Parliament in 1694, the immediate purpose of raising funds for the war against France, and the gradual assumption of central-bank responsibilities in the nineteenth century. William Paterson, the £1.2 million, banknotes, “the first central bank” and “copied worldwide” remain legacy copy; the page keeps the “legacy copy · source pending” label.

Read it by separating “why it was founded” from “what it later became”. The first was a wartime fiscal expedient; the second was more than two centuries of institutional evolution. Writing later functions back into 1694 makes history look too tidy. The real process looks more like an emergency loan that slowly grew into a system.

Original entry and sentence-level review

In 1694, Scottish merchant William Paterson obtained a royal charter to found the Bank of England in London.1,5 The bank lent £1.2 million to the government to support the war with France, and in return was authorized to issue banknotes.2 The Bank of England was the first true central bank, responsible for managing the national debt, issuing currency, and eventually supervising commercial banks.3 Its successful model was copied worldwide.4

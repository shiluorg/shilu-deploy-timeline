The short version

In 1765 Parliament passed the Stamp Act. The source is precise about what made it different: it was the first “internal” tax levied directly on the North American colonies by Parliament, not merely a duty collected at the ports. The tax fell on printed matter — newspapers, legal documents, licences, playing cards. The legacy entry supplies that list; the source does not itemise every category in the same passage, but the “direct internal tax” character is clear.

A few pence were not the point. The offence was procedural: Parliament claimed the right to choose taxes for the colonies, while the colonies claimed that their own assemblies should decide their own affairs. The argument began with sheets of paper and ended as a dispute over who had the right to rule.

Why it happened

The legacy entry puts the background down to debt from the Seven Years' War. That direction is sound: the empire had spent money on war and London wanted the colonies to cover part of the bill. The source confirms that the Stamp Act was Parliament's first direct internal tax on the colonies; it does not lay out the budget line by line. What is certain is that the tax was not an improvised levy but a statutory charge attached to printed paper.

Why did the stamp touch a nerve? Because it did not choose its victims. Merchants, lawyers, printers, tavern keepers and card players all met the stamp as soon as they handled paper. Imperial finance reached into the most ordinary corners of colonial life.

What happened

The legacy entry tells a lively story: colonists adopted the slogan “no taxation without representation”, formed the Sons of Liberty, burned stamps and beat tax collectors; in October 1765 nine colonies met in New York and adopted a declaration of rights; in March 1766 Britain repealed the act. The source confirms three nodes: the act in 1765, the meeting of nine colonies in New York in October, and the repeal in 1766. The slogan, the organisation's name, the burning and the beatings belong to the legacy narrative, not to a verified scene.

A safer reading is that the act forced the colonies onto one table. Nine colonies sending delegations to New York showed that protest had moved from a single port's riot to inter-colonial coordination. The declaration of rights was not chiefly about paying less; it was about who had the power to decide.

What changed

The act was repealed in 1766. The legacy entry says Britain gave way “under pressure”; that judgment is plausible, but the source confirms only the repeal, not the decision-making in London. A tax can be withdrawn; the constitutional argument cannot. If Parliament could legislate for the colonies, why could the colonies not send representatives to Parliament? If the colonies obeyed only their own assemblies, what held the empire together?

The legacy entry calls this struggle an important prelude to American independence. That is not an exaggeration, but the sharper point is that it put the conflict between parliamentary sovereignty and colonial consent on the table. Independence eleven years later, and the Philadelphia convention twelve years later, both answered the same questions: who may tax, who may represent, and who may say no.

How to read the evidence

This entry carries one source, Britannica's article on the Stamp Act. It directly supports three points: Parliament passed the act in 1765 as the first direct internal tax on the colonies; nine colonies met in New York in October 1765; the act was repealed in 1766. The list of taxable items, “no taxation without representation”, the Sons of Liberty, the burning of stamps and the beating of tax collectors remain legacy copy; the page keeps the “legacy copy · source pending” label.

Read it by separating “what the act was” from “what happened in the streets”, and then separating “the streets” from “independence”. The act has a source; the streets need newspapers, court records and letters; independence is a longer political process. Do not press all three into one smooth chain of cause and effect.

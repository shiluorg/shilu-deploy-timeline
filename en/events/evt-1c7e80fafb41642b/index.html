To remedy the weaknesses of the central government under the Articles of Confederation, 55 delegates from twelve states (Rhode Island refuses to attend) meet in Philadelphia for the Constitutional Convention. After 116 days of intense debate, they adopt the Constitution of the United States, establishing separation of powers (legislative, executive, judicial) and federalism. The Constitution provides for a president as head of state, a bicameral Congress, and a Supreme Court as the highest judicial authority. The Constitution goes into effect in 1788, and Washington is elected first president in 1789.

The Constitutional Convention was not a popular assembly but a negotiation among state elites. Representation for large and small states, federal versus state power and how slavery counted in population were all parts of the bargain.

The short version

Philadelphia, summer 1787, the Pennsylvania State House. It was hot outside; inside, the doors were shut and the session ran for months. The source leaves a clean set of numbers: May 25 to September 17, delegates from twelve states, 55 participants, 39 signatures on the Constitution. The legacy entry adds that the states chose 74 deputies in all and that Rhode Island refused to attend.

This was not a routine gathering. It had to answer the most awkward question facing a country that had just won its independence: each state had its own government, tariffs and militia, while the centre could barely make anyone do anything.

Why it happened

The legacy entry describes the background as the weakness of the central government under the Articles of Confederation. In practice that meant Congress could not tax directly, could not raise troops reliably, and could not settle trade disputes between states. After the war, debt, land and taxation conflicts surfaced; in Massachusetts, Shays' Rebellion broke out. Only then did the states agree to send delegates to Philadelphia to renegotiate the arrangement of power.

What the source confirms is the meeting itself: the dates, twelve states, 55 participants, 39 signers. Why those twelve came, and why Rhode Island stayed away, is only partly explained by the source; the legacy additions are background, not minutes.

What happened

The legacy entry describes 116 days of intense debate that produced separation of powers, federalism, a bicameral Congress and a Supreme Court. That summary captures the outcome, but it is not the source's text. The source confirms the dates and the numbers; it does not list the Constitution clause by clause.

The hard part was not whether to have a federation, but how to seat its parts. Large states wanted representation by population; small states wanted one vote per state. North and south argued over how enslaved people should be counted. Whether the executive should be one person, and whether he could be re-elected, had no ready answer. What emerged was a bundle of compromises, not a blueprint derived straight from principle. The legacy phrase “separation of powers” is a later summary; it does not replace the story of the bargaining.

What changed

The Constitution was ratified by the states and took effect in 1788; Washington was elected first president in 1789. The legacy entry joins the dots neatly. The source covers only the 1787 convention, not ratification or the first election. What can be confirmed is that 39 men signed on September 17 and that the convention produced a text to send to the states.

The Constitution did not solve every American problem at once. It created a federal government and preserved state power; it wrote rules for representation and elections without giving everyone the vote; it compromised over slavery and left the heaviest conflict to later generations. To say it “brought a nation into being” is not wrong, but it is more accurate to say that it fitted a loose confederation with a working frame, and left several sharp nails inside that frame.

How to read the evidence

This entry carries one source, Britannica's “Constitutional Convention”. It directly supports the dates May 25 to September 17, 1787, the twelve states, the 55 participants and the 39 signatures. The specific debates, the separation-of-powers clauses, ratification in 1788 and Washington's election in 1789 remain legacy copy pending further research; the page keeps the “legacy copy · source pending” label.

Read it by separating “how long the meeting lasted, who came, who signed” from “what the Constitution said and why”. The first has a source; the second still needs the convention records, delegates' letters and the constitutional text. Do not turn a later summary into words spoken in that Philadelphia room.

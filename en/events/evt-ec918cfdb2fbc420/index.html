The Rise of the Ottomans: From Frontier Beylik to Empire
Chapter 1 of 6
Next chapter1326: Bursa becomes the capital

Traditional chronology treats 1299 as the starting point of the Ottoman state, but the year was assigned by later dynastic historians. The more reliable picture is this: at the end of the 13th century, after the Mongols defeated the Seljuk army in 1293 and loosened the Seljuk order in Anatolia, Osman led ghazi raiders on the Bithynian frontier and by about 1300 controlled an area from Eskişehir to the plain of İznik.

This territory was not won in a single battle. It accumulated through frontier raiding, alliances and local Byzantine conflicts. Osman gathered fighters from different backgrounds and held their loyalty through plunder and land distribution.

“Ghazi” was not a unified regular army but a loose body of armed men around frontier opportunities. Its strengths were mobility, low cost and local knowledge; its weakness was that it dispersed after a campaign and could not easily hold territory.

So 1299 should not be written as a moment of “declaring independence and founding an empire.” It is a later starting point added to a frontier history; real state-building waited for Bursa.

Traditional chronology treats 1299 as the starting point of the Ottoman state. The year comes from later dynastic narrative; contemporary evidence more often describes Osman as a ghazi commander on the Bithynian frontier at the end of the 13th century.1

After the Mongols finally defeated the Seljuqs in 1293, several independent Turkmen principalities appeared in Anatolia. Osman was one of them; based at Söğüt, he raided the Byzantine frontier in Bithynia.2

By about 1300 Osman controlled an area from Eskişehir to the plain of İznik and had beaten several Byzantine attempts to curb him. He was still a frontier bey, not an emperor ruling Eurasia.3

The significance of 1299 lies less in a dramatic declaration of independence than in a long process: frontier war, migration and Byzantine civil strife pushed a small principality onto the historical stage.4

The Mongol Empire: From the Onon to the Volga
Chapter 12 of 12
Previous chapterLegnica: the destruction of the European host

In 1242 Batu's western army withdrew from Hungary and Poland to the south Russian steppe. This was not a rout after a tactical defeat but the result of the great khan's death: the armies of the various princes had to return and confirm the order of power.

Batu built Sarai on the lower Volga (near present-day Astrakhan, Russia) as the capital of the Golden Horde. The Golden Horde became one of the four khanates of the Mongol Empire and ruled the Rus' lands for two and a half centuries. The Volga thus became both the end point of this western line and the starting point of a new regime.

Looking back from 1206 to 1242, Mongol expansion rested on three things: the thousand-household system that turned population into armies, the keshig and princely command structure that could operate across tribes, and siege technology and craftsmen absorbed from conquered regions. At the same time, the killing, burning and forced movement that followed the fall of cities are part of this line and cannot be omitted. The turn at the Volga in 1242 shows that how far the empire could go depended not only on the battlefield but on who occupied the khan's seat.

Mongols capture Jin central capital Zhongdu

After Emperor Xuanzong moved the capital to Bianjing, the defense of Zhongdu had already become an abandoned task. Left behind were Wanyan Chenghui and Molian Jinzhong; faced with mutiny in the army, they could not maintain order. On 31 May 1215 Mongol forces broke into the outer city, and the remaining officials killed themselves rather than surrender.

After the fall the Mongols set fires in the city and burned the palace; the tablets of Jin emperors and the books in the imperial ancestral temple were destroyed together, and treasures including Liao dynasty regalia were carried off. The event record treats this moment as Jin's definitive abandonment of North China and the Mongols' rise to hegemony in East Asia.

Militarily, the fall of Zhongdu did not mean the end of Jin. The court remained at Bianjing, and resistance continued in Henan and Guanzhong; but the political center of North China had fallen, and the Mongols had acquired a base they could administer over the long term. The turn toward the west came after this stage.

Original entry and sentence-level review

After Emperor Xuanzong moved the capital to Bianjing, the Zhongdu garrison commanders Wanyan Cheng-hui and Mo-nian Jin-zhong were unable to defend the city due to a mutiny.1 On 31 May 1215 the Mongols broke through the outer walls, and the remaining Jin officials committed suicide.2 The Mongols set fire to the imperial palace, burned the ancestral tablets and books in the imperial temple, and plundered countless treasures, including those of the Liao dynasty.3 The fall of Zhongdu marked the complete Jin abandonment of North China, making the Mongol Empire the dominant power in East Asia.4

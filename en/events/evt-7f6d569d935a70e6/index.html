Hannibal crosses the Alps: bringing war into Italy

In spring 218 BCE Hannibal set out from Cartagena in Spain; the whole journey from Cartagena took five months.

The Alpine crossing itself took 15 days; when he descended into Italy he had about 25,000 infantry, 6,000 cavalry and most of his original 37 elephants.

Where the pass lay was never fixed by the ancient authors. Polybius and later Livy did not name it clearly; modern candidates include Montgenèvre, the Little St. Bernard, Mount Cenis, and the higher Col du Clapier–Savine Coche and Col de la Traversette.

Once in Italy, Hannibal won successively at the Ticino, the Trebbia (December 218 BCE) and Lake Trasimene (June 217 BCE); in 216 BCE he destroyed a large Roman army at Cannae. He never marched on Rome itself.

The Second Punic War ran from 218 to 201 BCE; although Hannibal won repeated battles in Italy, Rome ultimately prevailed and seized Carthage's territories in Spain. Hannibal was defeated by Scipio at Zama in 202 BCE, later went into exile, and died about 183–181 BCE.

Crossing the Alps was a logistical feat, not a strategic end point. Hannibal could win battles but could not break Rome's alliance system; the closer he came to Rome, the narrower his supply and political space became.

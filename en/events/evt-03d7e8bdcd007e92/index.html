Pasteur successfully develops rabies vaccine

In 1885 Louis Pasteur and his colleagues carried out the first human rabies vaccination.1 The patient was nine-year-old Joseph Meister, who had been severely bitten by a rabid dog.2 Pasteur treated him with a series of injections of progressively weakened rabies material, and Meister recovered fully after 13 injections. The case showed that a vaccine could be used after exposure, not only before it, and it gave practical shape to the emerging ideas of microbiology and immunology. The treatment attracted public attention and turned rabies from an almost certain death into a condition that medicine could confront. The episode also raised difficult questions about risk and the testing of new treatments on human patients. The case became a model for later vaccine campaigns.

The Law of the Twelve Tables is promulgated in Rome

Tradition places the making of the Twelve Tables in 451–450 BCE: a first board of ten commissioners drafted ten tables, a second board added two more, and the code was then posted in the Roman Forum, likely on bronze tablets. The original text does not survive intact; today it is reconstructed from quotations in later legal writings.

The immediate effect of a written code was to let plebeians know the rules and use them against arbitrary interpretation by patrician officials. Before that, judgments rested on unwritten custom preserved by a small group of learned patricians.

But the Twelve Tables were not a liberalizing reform. They recognized the prerogatives of the patrician class and the patriarchal family, allowed enslavement for unpaid debt, and let religious custom enter civil cases.

The code later came to be seen as a foundation of Roman private law, but it was not a modern bill of rights. It wrote the rules down and also wrote inequality into the rules.

The fall of Constantinople ends the Eastern Roman Empire

On April 6, 1453, Mehmed II began bombarding Constantinople; the siege lasted 55 days and the city fell on May 29. The Ottomans deployed about 60,000–80,000 soldiers and 69 cannon, including great bombards that fired stone balls.

The defenders numbered about 7,000, including Byzantine soldiers, Genoese and Venetian volunteers. The walls were bombarded from land and sea, the Golden Horn was sealed with a chain, and part of the Ottoman fleet was dragged overland into the inlet.

The final assault came on May 29, with the Ottomans breaking through near the Gate of St. Romanus. The Genoese commander Giovanni Giustiniani was mortally wounded and withdrew, and the defence lost its command chain; 3,000 Janissaries climbed the walls. Constantine XI died and the city fell.

The city was sacked for three days; Hagia Sophia became a mosque. Mehmed II did not move the capital there immediately; the transfer was completed in 1457. What ended in 1453 was the Eastern Roman Empire, not merely the defence of one city.

On April 6, 1453, the Ottoman army began bombarding Constantinople; the siege lasted 55 days and the city fell on May 29.1 Mehmed II blockaded the city by land and sea with about 60,000–80,000 soldiers and 69 cannon.2

The defenders repaired breaches on the walls at night. Giustiniani, a Genoese commander, was mortally wounded on the ramparts and withdrew, lowering morale; Mehmed II then sent about 3,000 Janissaries through the Gate of St. Romanus and took the inner wall.3

Constantine XI died. The city was sacked for three days; Mehmed II then stopped the looting, entered Hagia Sophia and converted it into a mosque.4

'Renamed Istanbul and made the capital at once' is a simplification. The city's population had fallen to about 40,000–50,000 in 1453; the Ottoman capital was transferred from Edirne to Constantinople in 1457.5

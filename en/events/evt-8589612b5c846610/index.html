Early administrative writing system in Mesopotamia

Around 3200 BCE late Uruk culture in Sumer developed a systematic proto-cuneiform script for economic management. A corpus of tablets from Uruk records the distribution of livestock, commodities and labour.

Writing allowed knowledge to travel across time and space, and made social management more complex. A person did not have to be present because a tablet could remember for him; a warehouse did not have to rely on oral tradition because a ledger could be checked. Administrative capacity now depended on a reproducible format of record, not only on a manager's memory.

These tablets are not literature but accounts, lists and receipts. They look dull, yet they explain the city better than any hero story: who delivered how much grain, who received livestock and cloth, who was taken for labour service.

Around 3200 BCE, Uruk produced administrative records written in proto-cuneiform.1 The signs on the tablets began as tokens and pictographs, used to register livestock, commodities and workers; one Uruk III tablet is thought to be a list of slaves' names.2 Later, many pictographs lost their original pictorial function, the sign inventory fell from about 1,500 to about 600, and writing became increasingly phonological.3 Writing's first job was accounting, not poetry: it allowed goods, labour and names to be checked across time.

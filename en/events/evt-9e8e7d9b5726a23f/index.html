Nicopolis: Bayezid I crushes an international crusade

In 1394 Pope Boniface IX called for a crusade; in 1396 French, Burgundian, Hungarian, Bohemian, Croatian, German and Hospitaller forces formed a coalition under Sigismund of Hungary. This was not a last crusade organised by the Duke of Burgundy, nor a relief expedition for Constantinople.

On September 25, 1396, the two armies met at Nicopolis (in present-day Bulgaria). The crusaders numbered about 12,000–15,000 against some 25,000 Ottoman troops under Bayezid I; Serbian vassals supplied about 5,000 men.

The crusaders' heavy cavalry broke the Ottoman front line in the first charge, but Bayezid kept his main force in reserve and used the Serbs to hold the flank. Crusader infantry and cavalry lost contact, and the counterattack routed them.

Sigismund was among the few who escaped. Bayezid then had most of the prisoners executed; the victory ended a massive international effort to halt Ottoman expansion in the Balkans.

But the victory did not last. In 1402 Timur defeated Bayezid at Ankara; the Ottoman state entered an interregnum and nearly split. Nicopolis proved the Ottomans could beat an international coalition, and also exposed their dependence on one strong sultan.

Original entry and sentence-level review

On September 25, 1396, Bayezid I defeated an international crusade nominally led by Sigismund of Hungary at Nicopolis in present-day Bulgaria. Pope Boniface IX called for the crusade in 1394, and France, Burgundy, Hungary, Bohemia, Croatia, Germany and the Knights Hospitaller sent contingents.1

The crusaders numbered about 12,000–15,000 against some 25,000 Ottoman troops. The French knights charged first and separated themselves from the Hungarian support; Bayezid committed his reserves and about 5,000 Serbian soldiers in a flanking counterattack that completed the encirclement.2

Sigismund was among the few who escaped. Bayezid then had most of the prisoners executed; the victory ended a massive international effort to halt Ottoman expansion in the Balkans.3

Nicopolis did not make Bayezid I invincible. In 1402 Timur defeated him at Ankara, and the Ottoman Empire entered an interregnum; Europe's memory of Nicopolis coexisted with this temporary collapse of the empire.4

    • Battle of Nicopolis (1396)Britannica, Battle of Nicopolis, opening paragraphs
      Battle of Nicopolis, a catastrophic military defeat for Christian knights at the hands of the Ottoman Turks on September 25, 1396. It brought an end to massive international efforts to halt Turkish expansion into the Balkans and central Europe. This development led Pope Boniface IX in 1394 to call for a crusade against the rising power of the Muslim Ottoman Turks in southeast Europe. In response to the pope's call, nobles from across Christian Europe eventually signed up for the venture, including large contingents from France, Burgundy, Hungary, Bohemia, Croatia, and Germany and from the Knights Hospitaller. Led by Sigismund, King of Hungary, they embarked on the long journey to the Danubian fortress of Nicopolis in modern-day Bulgaria.

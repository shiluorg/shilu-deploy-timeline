Solon's reforms in Athens lay foundation for democracy

In 594 BCE Athens faced severe wealth inequality and class conflict. The archon Solon introduced major reforms: the Seisachtheia cancelled all debts and debt-slavery; citizens were divided into four property classes with political rights corresponding to wealth; and the Council of Four Hundred and the people's jury courts were created.

This was not a reform that simply spoke for the poor. Solon neither fully satisfied the commoners nor preserved aristocratic privilege; he cancelled debts but did not turn the polis into a community of equals. The reforms wrote economic standing into political rights, matching different property classes to different levels of political participation while keeping the classes themselves.

The event record calls Solon's reforms the foundation of Athens' slave-owning democracy. Two words matter here: democracy meant distributing power inside the citizen collective; slave-owning meant that beyond that collective there were people excluded. Political participation widened from Solon onward, but the question of who was entitled to be a citizen did not disappear.

In 594 BCE, Facing severe wealth disparity and class conflict in Athens, the archon Solon (c. 638–558 BCE) enacted major reforms: the 'Seisachtheia' cancelling all debts and debt-slavery; dividing citizens into four property classes with political rights corresponding to wealth; and creating the Council of Four Hundred and the people's jury courts.1 Solon's reforms neither fully satisfied the commoners nor preserved aristocratic privilege, but they laid the groundwork for Athenian slave-owning democracy.2

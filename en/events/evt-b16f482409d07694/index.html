The alphabet: from North Semitic to Greek

The Phoenician alphabet developed out of the North Semitic alphabet and was spread around the Mediterranean by Phoenician traders; it is the probable ancestor of the Greek alphabet and therefore of all Western alphabets. It was not invented from nothing but was a writing tool repeatedly used in trade and administration.

The earliest surviving Phoenician inscription is the Ahiram epitaph at Byblos, dating from the 11th century BCE. It is written in the North Semitic alphabet. The Phoenician alphabet gradually developed from that prototype.

The alphabet had only 22 signs, written from right to left and representing consonants alone. It had no separate vowel letters, so readers supplied vowels from context. In Phoenicia proper it remained in use until about the 1st century BCE.

Colonies produced variants. Cypro-Phoenician was used from the 10th to the 2nd century BCE and Sardinian around the 9th century; a third variant evolved at Carthage into Punic and neo-Punic, written until about the 3rd century CE.

The Greek alphabet formed about 1000 BCE, derived from the North Semitic alphabet via the Phoenicians; the Greeks turned several consonantal signs into vowels. Alef, he, yod, ayin and vav became alpha, epsilon, iota, omicron and upsilon. The western Greek alphabet passed through Etruscan to Latin.

The point of alphabet history is not who invented it first, but who made a set of signs simple enough to be borrowed and rewritten by different languages.

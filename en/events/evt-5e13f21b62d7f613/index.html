Unified Silla: borrow Tang, then expel Tang

In 660 the allied forces of Silla and Tang destroyed Baekje; in 668 they destroyed Goguryeo. Silla thus unified the former territories of the three kingdoms, the first occasion in historical times that the Korean peninsula was unified under indigenous leadership.

But unification was not completed in one move. For nearly a decade afterwards Silla fought Tang and finally expelled its forces: a two-track operation of borrowing an army and then pushing it out.

After unification the country was reorganised on the Chinese bureaucratic model: ministries and bureaus were created, the peninsula was divided into nine administrative provinces, and garrisons were stationed across the country. Yet a bureaucracy based on merit was never firmly established, and the old aristocracy remained in control. “Unified Silla” was both a local re-integration of the peninsula and a survival technique that used and then rejected an outside empire.

Sources: 4 claims (3 sourced); Britannica “Unified Silla Dynasty”, the Silla–Tang alliance, the expulsion of Tang and the administrative reorganisation.

In 660 the allied forces of Silla and Tang destroyed Baekje; in 668 they destroyed Goguryeo. Silla thus unified the former territories of the three kingdoms, the first occasion in historical times that the Korean peninsula was unified under indigenous leadership1. For nearly a decade afterwards Silla fought Tang and finally expelled its forces2.

After unification the country was reorganised on the Chinese bureaucratic model: ministries and bureaus were created, the peninsula was divided into nine administrative provinces, and garrisons were stationed across the country. Yet a bureaucracy based on merit was never firmly established, and the old aristocracy remained in control3.

Unification was not an endpoint but a redefinition of who held power: Silla used Tang's armies to destroy its rivals, then fought Tang's garrisons out, and finally wrote the unification narrative as its own4.

      After the unification, the country was reorganized in accord with the Chinese bureaucratic model. A complex system of ministries and bureaus was created; the peninsula was divided into nine provinces, which were in turn divided into prefectures and subprefectures; and military garrisons were established throughout the country. But a bureaucratic class based on merit, as in China, was never firmly established, and the old aristocracy remained firmly in control.

The Hammurabi stele, c. 1750 BCE

Event
c. 1750 BCEBabyloniaLawSource-checked · see review scopeL2 · Source linked

Volume position: Mesopotamia · Ch. 5/7

Locate on the globe ↗Compare civilizations ↗More in this year ↗

The Hammurabi stele in the Louvre is carved from black basalt and stands about 2.25 metres high.1 Its inscription was cut around 1750 BCE and gathers nearly 280 judicial decisions.2 The text is conventionally called a code, but it is better understood as a collection of ancient legal decisions than as a modern comprehensive statute book. French excavations at Susa, in Iran, found the stele in 1902. It had been carried there centuries after it was made, as war booty, by the Elamite king Shutruk-Nahhunte. The Louvre has displayed it since 1904. The stele matters both as a legal record and as an object whose journey shows how monuments are moved, reused and given new meanings over time, and how law itself can be tied to a physical object.

In the eighth century BCE the Greek world went through the formation of the polis system. Mountains made land transport difficult, while natural harbours and islands divided the coast into many small units; Greeks organised themselves into independent political entities centred on a town and including the surrounding countryside. Each polis had its own laws, government and army, and the collective rule of citizens was treated as the core political idea.

The polis was not a modern state. It usually centred on a walled town, with farmland, pasture and villages around it; the units were small, but they placed politics, cult and war inside one community. The source record speaks of several hundred poleis, which means that “Greece” was never one unified state but many competing political units.

Athens, Sparta, Corinth, Thebes and other poleis rose one after another and became the basic units of classical Greek civilisation. They shared language, myth and festivals, but fought over borders, allies and hegemony. The polis gave citizens room to take part in public affairs; it also left inside the system the question of who counted as a citizen and who was excluded.

In the 8th century BC, the Greek world experienced the formation of the polis (city-state) system.1 Due to a mountainous geography that hindered land transport, along with numerous natural harbors and islands, Greece gradually developed independent political entities centered on a city and incorporating surrounding rural areas—the poleis.2 Each polis had its own laws, government, and army, with the collective rule of citizens as the core political concept.3 Athens, Sparta, Corinth, Thebes, and other poleis rose one after another, becoming the basic units of classical Greek civilization.4

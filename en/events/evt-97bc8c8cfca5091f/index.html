The Ottomans cross the Dardanelles and make Gallipoli a European bridgehead

In 1354 Süleyman Pasha, son of Orhan, crossed the Dardanelles and seized the Gallipoli peninsula; some references give about 1356. Gallipoli controlled the European side of the strait and was the Ottomans' first permanent foothold in Europe.

The Ottomans built a naval base there and used Gallipoli as a transit station between Rumelia and Anatolia. Troops, grain, taxes and intelligence moved along both shores.

The strait was not a water wall but a two-way channel. Once the Ottomans crossed it, they drew Balkan land, people and nobles into their network; Byzantium and Venice lost their exclusive hold on the northern shore.

Gallipoli mattered not because of one landing but because the Ottomans stayed. A permanent base turned raid into occupation, occupation into taxation, and taxation into the next army.

In 1354 Süleyman Pasha, son of Orhan, crossed the Dardanelles and took the Gallipoli peninsula. Some references give 'about 1356'; the difference comes from how the conquest is dated.1

Gallipoli was not an ordinary raid. It put Ottoman forces on the European side and became a permanent base for raids into the Balkans; it was later maintained as a naval base and as a transit station between Rumelia and Anatolia.2

From there the Ottomans intervened in the succession wars of Thrace, Macedonia and the Balkans. European land, taxation and vassals gradually mattered more than the Anatolian frontier.3

But a bridgehead is not an empire. It needed manpower, food and ships from the rear; if the straits were lost, the European foothold would become an isolated island.4
